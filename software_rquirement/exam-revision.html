Concepts

Open a card's slide definition when you need exact wording for an exam.

1 · The SDLC and its four phases

What it is. The life of a system, split into Planning (should we build it? what value? how managed?), Analysis (who uses it, what must it do, where and when), Design (architecture, database, programs), and Implementation (build, test, install, support). Each phase produces deliverables — documents or files that let the next phase proceed.

Why it matters

RE lives mainly in Analysis, but starts in Planning and re-enters Design. Placing it correctly is the first thing an examiner checks.

Example. Planning → feasibility study and project plan. Analysis → as-is model (paper logbooks, shared calendar), to-be model, requirements specification. Design → mobile app + web dashboard + EHR integration.

Exam trapTreating “SDLC” as a synonym for “waterfall”. The SDLC is the set of phases; waterfall is one methodology that orders them sequentially. Phases may run sequentially, incrementally, iteratively or in parallel.

Slide definitions (10, 12)
“The process consists of four phases. Each phase consists of a series of steps. Each phase produces deliverables (documents or files providing understanding about the project).”Slide 10

2 · Methodologies, in one ladder

What it is. A methodology is “a formalised approach to implementing the SDLC, unique on the order and focus it places on each phase”. The family tree: structured (waterfall: strict sequence, long deliverables, hard to go back; parallel: split the build into independent subprojects), RAD (phased: release versions, most important requirements in v1; prototyping: quick-and-dirty cycles that evolve into the product; throwaway: build a design mock-up, then discard it), agile (XP and Scrum), and DevOps (the dev↔ops infinity loop).

Why it matters

“Which methodology?” is the classic applied essay question. Justify with project characteristics, never with preference.

Example. Radiology system with fixed HL7/DICOM interfaces and a regulatory date → waterfall. Same appointment app rolled to five branches with different workflows → parallel development.

Exam trapPrototyping vs throwaway prototyping: prototyping evolves into the delivered system; throwaway is discarded after it settles the design. Also: XP allows change during an iteration; Scrum does not allow change during a sprint.

Slide definitions (21, 25, 27, 29, 35)
Waterfall: “Analysts and users proceed in sequence from one phase to the next… possible to go backward, but extremely difficult.”Slide 21
Throwaway: “Design prototype is created for users to better understand certain features or technical issues; not a working system… throw away design prototypes.”Slide 29

3 · Choosing a methodology

What it is. Seven methodologies scored Poor / Good / Excellent against six project characteristics: unclear user requirements, unfamiliar technology, complex systems, reliable systems, short time schedule, schedule visibility.

Why it matters

It turns “I like agile” into a defensible answer. There is no all-Excellent column — every choice is a trade-off.

Example. Vague requirements + 3-month deadline + visible progress: Phased, XP, Scrum all Excellent; Waterfall Poor on all three.

Exam trapForgetting schedule visibility as a criterion, and claiming one methodology is best for everything.

Matrix values (slide 40)
Unclear requirements: Waterfall Poor · Parallel Poor · Phased Good · Prototyping Excellent · Throwaway Excellent · XP Excellent · Scrum Excellent. Unfamiliar technology: Excellent only for Throwaway. Short schedule: Excellent for Phased, Prototyping, XP, Scrum; Good for Parallel and Throwaway.Slide 40

4 · Four process flow patterns

What it is. The same five activities — Communication, Planning, Modelling, Construction, Deployment — arranged four ways: linear (once, in order), iterative (loops back to repeat activities on the same increment), evolutionary (the whole cycle repeats, each pass ships an increment), parallel (streams run together, then merge).

Why it matters

Every methodology is a named arrangement of these flows. Classify it and its pros/cons follow.

Example. A booking module refined over several sprints = iterative. Version 1 to outpatients, version 2 adds doctors' schedules = evolutionary.

Exam trapCalling any loop “iterative”. Iterative = refine the same increment; evolutionary = produce successive increments.

5 · Why RE matters — the evidence

What it is. Four pieces of evidence: (1) CHAOS 2020 — 31% successful, 50% challenged, 19% failed; (2) success is social as well as technical — “good place, good team, good sponsor”; (3) the top failure factor is inaccurate requirements, followed by uninvolved sponsors; (4) the relative cost of fixing a defect grows 1–2 → 5 → 10 → 20 → 50 → 200 from requirements to maintenance.

Why it matters

This is the “motivation” half of the lesson. An essay that asserts importance without one number is weak.

Example. The pharmacy app ships without a queue-refresh interval. Patients see stale numbers; fixing the data feed after go-live costs about 200× writing the constraint during requirements.

Exam trapThe multipliers are relative, not money. And CHAOS measures project outcomes, not causation — do not say “31% succeed because of RE”.

6 · Problem domain vs solution domain

What it is. Requirements sit between a real-world problem and a software solution. The pyramid: Needs (problem domain, what people lack) → Features (what the product will offer) → Software requirements (solution domain, precisely what the software must do, and its constraints).

Why it matters

It explains why you cannot “just write requirements” from a brief. A change at the needs level propagates down every level — that is scope creep.

Example. Need: patients wait too long at the pharmacy. Feature: remote queue tracking. Requirement: “The app shall push a notification when the prescription is ready for pickup.”

Exam trapWriting screen layouts when the question asks for needs, or writing “patients should be happier” when it asks for requirements. Name the level you are at.

Slide 47
“Software requirements express the needs and constraints placed on a software product that contribute to the solution of some real-world problem.”Slide 47

7 · Inputs and outputs of the RE process exam

What it is. Five classic inputs — existing systems information, stakeholder needs, organisational standards, regulation, domain information — plus two the worked example adds: system constraints and assumptions. Three outputs: agreed requirements, a stakeholder/system/software specification, and a system model.

Why it matters

“Where do requirements come from?” is the most common short-answer question in this lesson, and the basis of Review Question 1.

Example (appointment system). Existing: paper ledgers, EHR, SMS gateway. Standards: ISO/IEC 27001, WCAG 2.1. Regulation: PDPA/GDPR, HIPAA. Domain: new/follow-up/procedure slots, shift patterns. Constraints: HL7 FHIR, 2 s at 500 users. Assumptions: ≤4 h training, 99.9% LAN uptime.

Exam trapMixing organisational standards (internal: ISO 27001, coding standards) with regulation (external law: PDPA, HIPAA). And forgetting domain information entirely.

8 · The five practical requirement classes exam

What it is. A course-specific classification used in both worked examples: general (records the system maintains), functional (what users can do), implementation (platform/construction constraints), performance (throughput, response time), usability (help, accessibility, layout).

Why it matters

Not a labelling question, she said. Use the five classes as a coverage checklist so no dimension is missed.

Example (slide 62). General: maintain records of bookings and no-show statistics. Functional: search slots by doctor, specialty, or date. Implementation: staff dashboard runs in a web browser with no local install. Performance: 50 transactions/second at peak. Usability: help menu and FAQ guide.

Exam trapCalling every “shall” statement functional. A statement about where the UI runs is implementation; how fast is performance; help or accessibility is usability.

Slide 61
“General requirements… Functional requirement… Implementation requirement… Performance requirement… Usability requirement.”Slide 61

9 · System RE vs software RE

What it is. Above software RE sits system engineering: system requirements engineering → architectural design → requirements partitioning (what is hardware, what is software, what is network) → software requirements engineering → sub-system development → system integration → system validation. The real difference is origin: system requirements come from user needs; software requirements come from system requirements.

Why it matters

It answers “why can't the software team just take the user's word?” — allocation must happen before software requirements can be written.

Example. System RE decides identity is verified by the existing MRN in the EHR, not a new biometric device. Partitioning assigns the reader to hardware and the booking logic to software. Software RE writes: “The system shall validate patient records using the existing 9-digit MRN.”

Exam trap“System = bigger, software = smaller” is wrong. Also learn the exception: when the system is all or almost all software, the software engineer effectively becomes the system engineer.

Slides 94–95
“The origin of system requirements lines in user needs while the origin of software requirements lines in the system requirements and/or specification.”Slide 95

10 · Stakeholders

What it is. “Anyone who is affected in some way by the system.” The deck names: customers (commission the software or represent the market), end users (operate it; often heterogeneous), managers of users, market analysts (proxy customers), external regulators, domain experts, software engineers, and requirements engineers / BAs (the bridge).

Why it matters

Every requirement conflict traces to two stakeholders with different views. Identifying them is a standard exam task.

Example. Hospital director = customer. Patients, receptionists, doctors = three different end-user sets. Medical records head = manager of users. PDPA regulator = external regulator. Senior nurse = domain expert.

Exam trapEquating “customer” with “end user”. The customer commissions or represents the market; the end user operates the system. And omitting regulators — in Digital Health they own the hardest constraints.

Slides 74–75
“Customers: This group comprises those who have commissioned the software or who represent the software's target market.” “External regulators: check system meets legal requirements.”Slides 74–75

11 · Six common RE problems and their cures

What it is. Each problem pairs with a practice, not a tool: volatility → Change Control Board + backlog grooming; ambiguous/conflicting requirements → templates, shared glossary, conflict workshops; unspoken implicit requirements → job shadowing, contextual inquiry, observation; communication/language barriers → visual models (wireframes, BPMN); gold plating → MoSCoW prioritisation against business KPIs; stakeholder unavailability → dedicated Product Owner, async feedback, management commitment.

Why it matters

This is Review Question 2, and the same list your quiz asked for. Answer with problem + cause + remedy.

Example. A surgeon refuses workshops. The team shadows the ward round and finds “appointment” includes pre-operative assessment slots nobody had mentioned.

Exam trapNaming the problem without the cause, or proposing “buy a better tool” where the deck proposes a practice. Volatility ≠ ambiguity: one changes, the other is unclear.

“Users fail to mention critical routine tasks or operational constraints during interviews because they take them for granted, resulting in a missing core functionality.”Slide 79

12 · The standards vocabulary

What it is. A requirement translates or expresses a need and its constraints and conditions. A constraint expresses measurable bounds imposed from outside. A condition is a measurable attribute stating when a requirement applies. A well-formed requirement is verifiable, needed, qualified by measurable conditions and bounded by constraints, and defines system capability — not a capability of the user. SRS = software and its external interfaces; SyRS = the system, its operational environments and external interfaces.

Why it matters

These definitions are the grading currency of the whole course. SRS vs SyRS is a favourite one-mark distinction.

Example. Poor: “The app should be fast.” Well-formed: “The system shall render the doctor's master schedule within 2 seconds at 500 concurrent staff users.”

Exam trapDefining a requirement as “what the user wants”. The standard stresses constraints and conditions, and the what-not-how rule. A user capability (“the nurse shall read the screen”) is not a system requirement.

Slides 86–89
“A well-formed requirement is a statement that can be verified… is qualified by measurable conditions and bounded by constraints, and defines the performance of the system… but not a capability of the user, operator, or other stakeholder.”Slide 89

13 · Verification vs validation

What it is. Validation checks that the system satisfies specified requirements — did we build the right system? Verification checks that each development phase satisfies the conditions imposed at its start — did we build it right? At requirements level (ISO 29148): requirements validation = the requirements define the right system as intended by stakeholders; requirements verification = the requirements are well formed.

Why it matters

It is one mark in almost every exam, and it changes what “testing a requirement” means: review the statement vs confirm the statement with users.

Example. Verification: “2 s at 500 users” is measurable, single-meaning, and does not conflict with 50 TPS. Validation: the pharmacy manager confirms 2 s solves the queue problem.

Exam trapThe classic inversion. Memorise: validation = right system; verification = built right / well formed.

Slide 91
“requirements validation*: confirmation by examination that requirements (individually and as a set) define the right system as intended by the stakeholders.” “requirements verification*: confirmation… that requirements… are well formed.”Slide 91

14 · ConOps vs OpsCon

What it is. ConOps = a verbal and graphic statement, in broad outline, of an organisation's assumptions or intent regarding an operation or series of operations; it bounds the operating space, capabilities, interfaces and environment. OpsCon = the same statement for the operation of a specific system or set of systems, from the users' and operators' perspective — architecture, data flow, operational modes and failovers.

Why it matters

They bridge the business case and the SRS, and students routinely merge them.

Example. ConOps: the business problem (long phone waits, no-shows), objectives (cut no-shows 35%), user environment (patients on public mobile networks). OpsCon: mobile app + web app, cloud microservices, MRN validation flow, degraded offline mode, PDPA and 50 TPS constraints.

Exam trapWriting an OpsCon that is all business goals (that is a ConOps) or all class diagrams (that is design documentation). The line is organisation/operations vs specific system from the user's perspective.

15 · Requirements management and tool support exam

What it is. The activities that ensure requirements are identified, documented, maintained, communicated and traced through the life cycle. Includes configuration management, traceability, impact analysis and version control. Tool support is a requirements database fed by converters, with a browser and query system, change control, report generator and backward and forward traceability. Two tool types: modelling/validation tools and management tools.

Why it matters

It closes the loop on change: RE needs a mechanism, not good intentions.

Example. Pharmacy asks for WhatsApp notifications after sign-off. Change control records it; forward traceability finds affected design and test items; backward traceability finds the originating need; the CCB decides.

Exam trapTreating requirements management as “keeping a spreadsheet”. And describing traceability only forward — the deck requires backward and forward.

16 · Requirements and design are interleaved

What it is. The deck states the ideal and then denies its practicality: “Requirements and design are interleaved. They should, ideally, be separate processes but in practice this is impossible.” RE costs about 15% of system development cost. If requirements are wrong: late, over budget, unreliable, unmet expectations, and very high maintenance costs.

Why it matters

These two lines are the most quotable close for an essay, and the interleaving explains Lesson 2's iterative/recursive material.

Example. Designing the appointment database reveals that “slot” must distinguish new-patient, follow-up and procedure durations. Design work produced a requirement.

Concepts

Cards 1–7 build the classification; 8–13 build the quality test; 14–16 explain how the set grows.

1 · The requirement hierarchy

What it is. RE produces a hierarchy of levels: stakeholder requirements → system requirements → system-element (software) requirements. Each level is derived from the one above and written for a different reader.

Why it matters

The hierarchy is the contract between business and engineering, and the basis for verification and acceptance.

Example. Board: patients should not wait >15 min at check-in → system: confirm arrival within 30 s of QR scan → software: “The QR module shall decode a code within 500 ms and return the patient ID.”

Exam trapCollapsing all levels into one list, or writing database table names in a stakeholder requirement.

Slide 6
“The result of requirements engineering is a hierarchy of requirements that: enables an agreed understanding between stakeholders… is validated against real-world needs, can be implemented; provides a basis of verifying designs and accepting solutions.”Slide 6

2 · Four independent category axes

What it is. The same requirement set can be sorted four ways: (1) by level — user / system / software; (2) by what vs how the project runs — product / project (process); (3) by behaviour vs constraint — functional / non-functional; (4) by boundary crossed — interface requirements.

Why it matters

“Classify this requirement” needs you to name the axis first. One requirement can sit on several axes at once.

Example. “The system shall send the booking to the FHIR server within 5 s” is a system requirement, a product requirement, functional, and an external interface requirement — all at once.

Exam trapTreating the axes as one taxonomy with one right answer. State the axis you are using.

Slide 7
“1. User, system and software requirements; 2. Product and process requirements; 3. Functional and non-functional requirements; 4. Interface requirements.”Slide 7

3 · User, system and software requirements

What it is. User requirements = the requirements of the system's customers/end-users. System requirements = the system as a whole: user requirements + other stakeholders (e.g. regulators) + requirements with no identifiable human source. Software requirements = derived from system requirements when the system contains software. A system is “an interacting combination of elements… hardware, software, firmware, people, information, techniques, facilities, services”.

Why it matters

Regulators and law are stakeholders with no individual voice — in Digital Health that is the whole game.

Example. Doctors want a fast prescribing screen (user). PDPA and the pharmacy committee require an audit trail (system, no single owner). Software: “The prescribing module shall write an immutable audit record of user, patient, drug, dose, timestamp.”

Exam trap“System requirements = software requirements.” System includes people, hardware and process. And user needs are not yet requirements — they must be transformed.

Slide 8
“System requirements, by contrast, encompass user requirements, requirements of other stakeholders (such as regulatory authorities), and requirements without an identifiable human source.”Slide 8

4 · Product vs project (process) requirements

What it is. Product requirements describe what is built: features, performance, quality. Project (process) requirements constrain how it is built and delivered: cost, schedule, staffing, test environments, data migration, training, maintenance, law, corporate policy.

Why it matters

A project requirement can fail acceptance but it is not a feature. Mixing them distorts prioritisation and estimation.

Example. Product: “encrypt patient data at rest with AES-256.” Project: “develop with two-week sprints.” Project: “migrate 10 years of legacy records before go-live.”

Exam trapMarking “the code must be written in Python 3.11” as functional. It is a project/technology constraint. “Load pages under 2 s” is product (performance), not project.

Slide 11
“Product requirements focus on what is being built, while project requirements focus on how it will be built and delivered… also called process requirements.”Slide 11

5 · Functional vs non-functional (+ the two NFR children)

What it is. Functional requirements describe functions or tasks the system performs — capabilities. Non-functional requirements constrain the solution: they split into technology constraints (mandate or prohibit named platforms, languages, browsers, database engines) and quality-of-service constraints (response time, throughput, accuracy, reliability, scalability).

Why it matters

Functional decides whether the system does the job; NFRs decide whether anyone can use, trust and afford it — and they drive architecture.

Example. Functional: patient books with a chosen doctor. Technology: “shall run on the hospital's Oracle 19c.” QoS: “99.9% monthly availability, excluding the 00:00–01:00 maintenance window.”

Exam trap“The system shall be secure” is not an NFR — it is a wish. And “implemented as a web app” is a technology constraint, not functional.

Slides 13–16
“System functional req.: describe the system or system element functions or tasks to be performed or executed… sometimes known as software capabilities.” “Nonfunctional requirements acts as constraint on a software solution.”Slides 13–14

6 · The three kinds of interface requirement

What it is. External — how the system talks to outside systems (payment gateway, maps, SMS/SMTP, ERP, FHIR). Internal system-element — how elements inside the system interact (ATM dispenser, barcode→POS, parking barrier). Internal human — how human roles interact through the system (physician → pharmacist → nurse; employee → manager; user → technician).

Why it matters

Interfaces are where integration projects fail. In Digital Health almost every requirement is an interface requirement.

Example (slide 21). Physician submits e-prescription → routed to pharmacist's dispensing queue for safety validation → pharmacist logs digital clearance → nurse's administration dashboard updates.

Exam trapWriting only external interfaces and forgetting human-to-human workflow. And using “instantly” — an interface requirement still needs a measurable bound.

Slide 18
“Interface requirements are the definition of how the system is required to interact with external systems… or how system elements is required to interact within the system, or how human elements, interact with each other.”Slide 18

7 · Turning needs into requirements

What it is. Requirements begin as stakeholder intentions — needs, goals, objectives — and become formal statements through two aids: the Concept of Operations (organisation-level understanding) and the System Operational Concept (system perspective). The output is structured stakeholder requirements.

Why it matters

The gap between “patients should wait less” and a testable requirement is closed by understanding operations, not by writing style.

Example. Need: reduce missed appointments. ConOps: outpatients phone the desk, 30% no-show, reminders are manual. OpsCon: automated SMS/Line reminder 24 h before with one-tap reschedule. Requirement: send reminder 24 h before and record delivery status.

Exam trapJumping from need to solution (“build an app”), which encodes the solution and violates implementation free.

Slide 22
“Defining requirements begins with stakeholder intentions (referred to as needs, goals, or objectives), that evolve into a more formal statement before arriving as valid stakeholder requirements.”Slide 22

8 · Well-formed requirement — the four clauses exam

What it is. A requirement is well formed when it (a) can be verified; (b) must be met or possessed by the system to solve a stakeholder problem; (c) is qualified by measurable conditions and bounded by constraints; (d) defines system performance/capability — not a capability of the user, operator or stakeholder.

Why it matters

Clause (d) is the most examined and most failed. It distinguishes a system obligation from a human duty.

Example. Bad: “The nurse shall check drug interactions.” Well formed: “The system shall display an alert within 1 second when a prescribed drug interacts with any active medication.”

Exam trapMemorising “verifiable, measurable, bounded” and forgetting “not a capability of the user”. Also: conditions, constraints and design decisions are attributes, not the requirement.

Slide 23
“A well-formed requirement is a statement that can be verified, has to be met or possessed by a system to solve a stakeholder problem or to achieve a stakeholder objective, is qualified by measurable conditions and bounded by constraints, and defines the performance of the system… but not a capability of the user, operator, or other stakeholder.”Slide 23

9 · Syntax: the pattern, the modals, the voice

What it is. A natural-language requirement should have a subject + verb + complement. Three reusable patterns: [Condition] [Subject] [Action] [Object] [Constraint]; [Condition] [Action or Constraint] [Value]; [Subject] [Action] [Value]. Modals: shall = mandatory; will = fact/futurity (avoid); should = preference; may = allowance; is/are = description. Avoid must, negatives, and passive voice.

Why it matters

One word changes the legal status of a clause, and therefore what can be tested at acceptance.

Example. “When a patient checks in by QR [Condition], the appointment system [Subject] shall set the status to Arrived [Action/Object] within 2 seconds [Constraint].”

Exam trapUsing “should” in a requirements table (slide 44 requirement 1 does exactly this as a teaching point), and passive “shall be able to export” instead of “shall export”.

Slides 24–26
“Requirements are mandatory binding provisions (เงื่อนไข) and use ‘shall’… Preferences… use ‘should’… Suggestion… use ‘may’.”Slide 25

10 · Conditions vs constraints

What it is. A condition is a measurable qualitative or quantitative attribute attached to a requirement that says when/how it applies (“under 500 concurrent users”). A constraint is an externally imposed limitation on the system, its design/implementation, or the development process (law, budget, platform, existing interfaces, physical size, operator capability, maintenance strategy).

Why it matters

Conditions make a requirement testable; constraints bound the solution space before design begins.

Example. Condition: “during regular service hours.” Constraint: “Patient data shall stay in Thailand (PDPA)” or “budget ≤ 5 million baht.”

Exam trapUsing the words interchangeably. A condition qualifies when/how; a constraint is a limit you cannot negotiate away.

Slide 27
“Conditions are measurable qualitative or quantitative attributes that are stipulated (กำหนดเงื่อนไข) for a requirement… Constraints externally imposed limitation on the system, its design, or implementation…”Slide 27

11 · The nine individual characteristics exam

What it is. One requirement must be: Necessary (essential, not obsolete), Implementation free (what, not how), Unambiguous (one interpretation), Consistent (no conflict), Complete (measurable, no amplification), Singular (one requirement, no conjunctions), Feasible (achievable within constraints), Traceable (up to need, down to design), Verifiable (means exist to prove it).

Why it matters

This list is the marking rubric for every requirement you will ever write.

Example. “ATM card and PIN” fails singular; “100% detection accuracy” fails feasible; “24×7 availability + nightly all-user maintenance” fails consistent.

Exam trap“Traceable” answered as “has an ID”. The ID enables traceability; the trace is the documented parent/child link. And do not add “prioritised” — priority is an attribute, not an individual characteristic.

Slides 29–33
“Singular. The requirement statement includes only one requirement with no use of conjunctions.” “Verifiable. The requirement has the means to prove that the system satisfies the specified requirement.”Slides 32–33

12 · The four set characteristics (and creep)

What it is. The whole set must be Complete (everything pertinent; no TBD/TBS/TBR), Consistent (no contradictions, no duplicates, one term per item), Affordable (satisfiable within life-cycle constraints), Bounded (stays inside scope). Failure produces requirements creep — uncontrolled growth that hits cost, schedule and quality.

Why it matters

A set can consist of nine perfect requirements and still fail because a consent requirement is missing or two availability targets contradict.

Example. Complete: no “[TBD]”. Consistent: “patient” is never “client” in one section and “customer” in another. Affordable: drop 99.999% because the hospital cannot fund it. Bounded: no billing module — billing is external.

Exam trapCalling an individual requirement “affordable” or “bounded”. These are set properties. And an unresolved placeholder means the set is not complete, however good the prose.

Slides 34–35
“Complete. The set of requirements needs no further amplification… contains no To Be Defined (TBD), To Be Specified (TBS), or To Be Resolved (TBR) clauses.”Slide 34

13 · Nine types of vague term

What it is. The diagnostic checklist: superlatives (best), subjective language (user-friendly), vague pronouns (it, this), ambiguous adverbs/adjectives (significant, minimal), open-ended terms (provide support, but not limited to), comparatives (better than), loopholes (if possible, as appropriate), incomplete references (no date/version), negative statements.

Why it matters

Nearly every bad requirement in the deck is bad for one of these nine reasons. Memorise the categories, not the examples.

Example. “User friendly” → subjective. Rewrite: first-time user completes a booking under 3 minutes, and the interface meets WCAG 2.1 AA.

Exam trap“Fixing” a vague term with another vague term (“fast” → “responsive”). Every rewrite needs a number, a named standard/version, or a test method.

Slide 38
“Vague and general terms shall be avoided. They result in requirements that are often difficult or even impossible to verify or may allow for multiple interpretations.”Slide 38

14 · The eight requirement attributes

What it is. Metadata on each requirement: Identification (unique, never changed, never reused), Priority (consensus 1–5 or H/M/L), Dependency (links, including low-priority requirements essential to others), Risk, Source (originator; ownership), Rationale (why it exists), Difficulty (Easy/Nominal/Difficult), Type (functional, non-functional, product, process).

Why it matters

Attributes drive prioritisation, change-impact analysis, risk management and cost estimation. ID + source + dependency are the machinery of traceability.

Example (slide 44). “Patient searches for a doctor” — type functional, source Patient/BA, dependency R6 (register), difficulty easy/nominal.

Exam trap“Priority means optional.” The slide denies this explicitly: priority identifies trade-space candidates, not unnecessary requirements.

Slide 40
“Once assigned, the identification has to be unique – it is never changed… nor is it reused…” “The priority is not intended to imply that some requirements are not necessary.”Slide 40

15 · Seven ways to organise functional requirements

What it is. By feature (call forwarding), system mode (training/normal/emergency), user class (elevator: passengers, maintenance, firefighters), object (patient monitoring: patients, sensors, nurses, rooms), stimulus (automatic landing: power loss, wind shear), response (paychecks, employee list), functional hierarchy (common inputs/outputs, DFDs).

Why it matters

Organisation is not cosmetic: it determines whether reviewers can see gaps.

Example. A hospital module organised by user class makes a missing “ward clerk” section obvious; organised by feature the same gap is invisible.

Exam trapAssuming one correct organisation. Choose the one that makes missing requirements and interfaces visible.

Slide 45
“Different systems may have requirements organized in different ways… Feature… System mode… User class… Object… Stimulus… Response… Functional hierarchy.”Slide 45

16 · Iterative vs recursive (the level distinction)

What it is. Iterative (ทำซ้ำ) = the same process repeated at the same level of the system. Recursive (เรียกซ้ำ) = the same set of processes applied to successive lower levels of system elements. Related: requirements are allocated to elements in parallel with architecture, which creates derived requirements; some requirements can only emerge after architecture exists.

Why it matters

It explains why requirements are not a one-pass phase, and why a requirement may legitimately mention a higher-level design decision.

Example. Iterative: three passes over the parking system's requirements at whole-system level. Recursive: then the same process inside the “entering gate” element, deriving requirements for reader, camera, barrier.

Exam trap“Iteration = doing it again, recursion = more detail” without the level. The discriminator is same level vs successive lower level, and recursion re-applies the same process set, not just decomposition.

Slides 46, 48
“When the application of the same process or set of processes is repeated on the same level of the system, the application is referred to as iterative (ทำซ้ำ).” “When the same set of processes… are applied to successive levels (lower level) of system elements… recursive (เรียกซ้ำ).”Slides 46, 48

From vague to well formed

Splitting a combined registration requirement

Before: “The system shall allow students to register for courses and shall send a confirmation email and shall update the timetable.” Two faults. It is not singular — three obligations joined by “and”. And “confirmation email” is premature design: the channel is a design choice, the obligation is to confirm. After: R1 “The system shall register a student in a course when the prerequisite is met and a seat remains.” R2 “The system shall notify the student of the registration outcome within one minute of the request.” R3 “The system shall update the course timetable within five seconds of a successful registration.” Three requirements, three tests, no channel named.

ก่อนแก้: “ระบบต้องให้นักศึกษาลงทะเบียนได้ และต้องส่งอีเมลยืนยัน และต้องอัปเดตตารางเรียน” ผิดสองข้อ: ไม่ singular เพราะรวมสามหน้าที่ด้วย “และ” และ “อีเมลยืนยัน” เป็น premature design เพราะช่องทางเป็นเรื่อง design ส่วนหน้าที่คือการยืนยัน. หลังแก้: R1 ลงทะเบียนได้เมื่อผ่าน prerequisite และที่ยังว่าง; R2 แจ้งผลภายใน 1 นาที; R3 อัปเดตตารางเรียนภายใน 5 วินาที — สามข้อ สามการทดสอบ ไม่ระบุช่องทาง

Removing premature design from an ATM requirement

Before: “The system shall use an Oracle stored procedure to debit the account and display a JavaScript alert when funds are insufficient.” Both halves are premature design: a database mechanism and a UI control, chosen before the requirement is settled. After: “The system shall debit the account exactly once per completed withdrawal, and shall reject the transaction when the balance is below the requested amount plus the fee, telling the customer the available amount.” Now it states an invariant (exactly once), a condition (below the amount plus fee), and an observable outcome — none of which mention Oracle or JavaScript.

ก่อนแก้: “ระบบต้องใช้ Oracle stored procedure ในการตัดบัญชี และแสดง JavaScript alert เมื่อเงินไม่พอ” ทั้งสองส่วนเป็น premature design — กลไกฐานข้อมูลและตัวควบคุม UI ถูกเลือกก่อนความต้องการจะชัด. หลังแก้: “ระบบตัดบัญชีหนึ่งครั้งต่อการถอนที่สำเร็จ และปฏิเสธรายการเมื่อยอดคงเหลือต่ำกว่าจำนวนที่ขอรวมค่าธรรมเนียม พร้อมแจ้งยอดที่ใช้ได้” — มี invariant (หนึ่งครั้ง) เงื่อนไข และผลที่สังเกตได้ โดยไม่เอ่ยเครื่องมือ

Where elicitation sits

One quadrant of the requirements process, not the whole process.

Fig. 3.1. After Cleland-Huang. The exam point: elicitation produces a draft; analysis and negotiation make it agreed. ตาม Cleland-Huang: elicitation ได้ ฉบับร่าง ส่วน analysis และ negotiation ทำให้เป็นข้อตกลง

Concepts

1 · What elicitation is

What it is. The activity whose aim is “discovering ‘the requirements’ of the system”, by working with customers and end users, studying documents and domain knowledge, and performing market studies. It is a knowledge-transfer process: the engineer elicits and documents existing customer knowledge and need. Also called capture, discovery, acquisition. It is communication, mediated by the requirements specialist between the users' domain and the engineer's technical world.

Why it matters

Every later artefact inherits elicitation errors. A missed ward workflow becomes a wrong use case and a system that fails in practice.

Example. For an appointment system it is not “ask the doctor what screens he wants”. It is discovering that appointments are part of out-patient care, interface with registration and diagnosis, and that doctors record availability 8 AM–5 PM.

Exam trap“Elicitation = writing the SRS” or “= interviewing”. Its output is a draft that still needs analysis, conflict resolution and negotiation.

Slides 7, 9
“It is a knowledge transfer process where req. engineer elicits and documents existing customer knowledge/need.” “Requirements specialists must mediate between the domain of the software users… and the technical world of the software engineer.”Slides 7, 9

2 · Problem analysis comes first

What it is. “The activity that encompasses learning about the problem to be solved (often through brainstorming and/or questioning), understanding the needs of potential users, trying to find out who the user really is, and understanding all the constraints of the solution.” Its goal is understanding the problem before development begins.

Why it matters

Elicit against the wrong problem and every technique gives high-quality answers to the wrong question.

Example. A clinic asks for an “AI triage app”. Problem analysis finds the real defect is that paper forms omit owner name and area code, causing duplicate records and failed reminders.

Exam trapJumping to a solution. Problem analysis is about the problem and constraints, not the system.

Slide 8
“Problem analysis is ‘the activity that encompasses learning about the problem to be solved… understanding the needs of potential users, trying to find out who the user really is, and understanding all the constraints of the solution.’”Slide 8

3 · Scope is the critical element

What it is. Informing project scope means describing the software and its purpose, and prioritising deliverables so the customer's most important business needs are satisfied first. The description must be scalable and extensible to accept later requirements that stay compatible, which needs a way to organise and classify requirements. Elicitation also supports early validation: stakeholders confirm the statements capture their needs.

Why it matters

Without a scope boundary every interview becomes an invitation to add features.

Example. “Phase 1 covers out-patient booking, cancellation and reminders, integrated with registration; it excludes billing, in-patient scheduling and telemedicine.”

Exam trapTreating scope as one sentence written at kick-off. It needs a baseline and a change-control mechanism.

Slide 10
“A critical element of requirements elicitation is informing the project scope… prioritizing the deliverables to ensure the customer's most important business needs are satisfied first.”Slide 10

4 · The twelve sources and issues

What it is (ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148). Goals · mission profile · organisational environment · operational environment and context of use · domain knowledge · operational scenarios · operational life cycle · operational deployment · stakeholders · business rules · performance · effectiveness (with efficiency). Plus potential system constraints: unchangeable interfaces, physical size, national law, duration/budget, existing platform, user capability.

Why it matters

Techniques find people's statements; requirements also live in goals, documents, law, interfaces, workload and the calendar.

Example (LMS table). Goals → centralised course delivery. Business rules → 10% late penalty per day, Module 1 before Module 2. Performance → 10,000 concurrent quiz submissions, page load <1.5 s. Effectiveness → 95% satisfaction, zero downtime during exams.

Exam trapPerformance ≠ effectiveness ≠ efficiency. Performance = speed of input/transfer/processing/storage/output. Effectiveness = accuracy and completeness of goal achievement. Efficiency = minimum resource consumption.

Slides 25–27
“Business rules… are statements that define or constrain some aspect of the structure or the behavior of the business itself.” “Effectiveness (ประสิทธิผล) means accuracy and completeness with which users achieve specified goal.” “Efficiency means… with minimum consumption of resources.”Slides 25–26

5 · Stakeholder identification and analysis

What it is. Stakeholders are “people who are affected by the system” — users, operators, supporters, developers, producers, trainers, maintainers, acquirers/suppliers, interfacing parties, regulators, society. Identify all classes and multiple organisational levels, because one class or level is biased. Where direct contact is impractical, use proxy stakeholders. The analysis template records: class, role/responsibility, expectations, constraints on capability/availability, and work processes + existing systems.

Why it matters

Conflicts come from roles, not from “users”. Missing a class guarantees a late, expensive discovery.

Example (slide 24). Doctor: provides care, records availability, constrained to 8 AM–5 PM. Patient: makes an appointment, views information. Both depend on registration and diagnosis systems.

Exam trap“Users” is not a stakeholder list. Separate the stakeholder from the requirement source (a policy manual is a source, not a person).

Slide 23
“Information gathered from only one stakeholder class, or only one level, is likely to be biased from a single perspective. A representative cross-section of stakeholders is necessary to provide the true picture of the ‘problem to be solved’.”Slide 23

6 · The seven elicitation problems exam

What it is. Fragmented domain knowledge · communication gap · volatility · low engagement · unarticulated needs · organisational politics · scope creep. Each has a cause and three practices as a cure (see the table below).

Why it matters

Your quiz asked for exactly this: two problems plus solutions. Answer with problem + cause + remedy.

Example. “Unarticulated needs”: a nurse says the system should “just know” which bed is free. Job shadowing shows a whiteboard and a phone call; 5 Whys shows the need is a single source of truth updated at discharge.

Exam trapMerging low engagement (availability/resistance) with communication gap (vocabulary/context). There are seven problems; slide 39 merely repeats slide 37.

7 · The four-stage process and its hand-off

What it is. (1) Establish objectives — business goals, problem, system constraints. (2) Understand background — organisational structure, application domain, existing system. (3) Organise knowledge — stakeholder identification, goal prioritisation, filtering irrelevant domain knowledge, collating against a checklist/ontology. (4) Collect requirements — stakeholder, domain and organisational requirements. Output: a draft document, then analysis for problems/conflicts/overlaps, then negotiation.

Why it matters

Explains why elicitation is never one meeting, and why a draft is not an agreement.

Example. Objectives: cut missed appointments 20%, integrate registration. Background: clinic workflow, paper diary. Organise: doctor/patient/receptionist/IT; discard facilities knowledge. Collect: interviews, documents, observation. Then analyse a conflict — 15-minute slots vs 10 — and negotiate.

Exam trapListing the four stage names without their contents. Know what happens between “collecting knowledge” and “collecting stakeholder requirements”: knowledge organisation.

Slides 42–43
“The large amount of knowledge which has been collected in the previous stage must be organized… and collated (ex. against checklist).” “There must be a negotiation stage to earn the agree requirements.”Slides 42–43

8 · Interviews

What it is. Five steps: (1) select interviewees and build a schedule by information need, spanning senior managers, mid-level managers, staff and users; (2) design closed, open and probing questions; (3) prepare — unstructured vs structured, anticipate answers, brief the interviewee; (4) conduct — top-down or bottom-up, record everything, summarise, separate opinions from facts, promise nothing; (5) follow up with a report within 48 hours, sent to the interviewee to verify.

Why it matters

Highest depth of information, and the main way to explore as-is problems and to-be expectations. Weak on breadth and integration.

Example. Open: “Walk me through a typical morning.” Probing: “Why do you phone the ward instead of using the system?” Closed: “How many appointments are rescheduled per day?”

Exam trapOmitting the 48-hour verified report — it is part of the technique, not paperwork, and it is early validation. Never present an opinion as a fact.

Slides 46, 48–49
“Prepare an interview report within 48 hours.” “Send it to the interviewee to verify.” “Separate opinions from facts.”Slide 46

9 · JAD / JRD

What it is. “Structured workshops with brainstorming” where managers, users and developers identify requirements together, usually in a prepared room, for hours to weeks. Roles: facilitator — guides but does not participate, remains neutral; scribe — prepares material, records, writes the report. Process: select participants (mixed levels, expert facilitator) → design the session (5–10 days over 3 weeks, prepared questions, structured, top-down) → prepare participants → conduct with ground rules → post-session report within 1–2 weeks.

Why it matters

Highest integration and user involvement. It is the technique for conflicting department rules and fast scope bounding.

Example. Cardiology, paediatrics and radiology disagree on slot rules. A 3-day JAD produces one slot model, a list of open conflicts (overbooking), and a signed scope boundary.

Exam trapThe facilitator does not participate. True conflict is listed as an open issue and escalated — not decided by the facilitator. And JAD's strength is integration, not breadth.

Slide 52
“JAD is structured workshops with brainstorming.” “Facilitator: guides the discussion but does not join as a participant.” “Scribe: prepare the meeting material, record the meeting discussion content, create the meeting report.”Slide 52

10 · Questionnaires

What it is. “A set of written questions used to obtain information from individuals”, paper or electronic. Use for large numbers of people, facts plus opinions, and external users. Typical response rates: <50% paper, <30% web. Process: select a representative sample → design (remove ambiguity, separate facts from opinions, plan the analysis) → administer (purpose, deadline, incentive, personal handout, non-respondent contact) → follow up with results and thanks. Design: easy questions first, logical sections, no crowding, consistent style, no abbreviations, no leading items, pretest, anonymity.

Why it matters

Highest breadth at lowest cost. Weak on depth, integration and follow-up — you cannot probe an ambiguous answer.

Example. 500 patients across three clinics: 10 items on booking frequency, channel, wait, preferred reminder, satisfaction — with a paper option for elderly patients.

Exam trapUsing a questionnaire for depth or conflict resolution, and forgetting non-response bias.

Slides 57, 59
“Typical response rates: < 50% (paper); < 30% (Web).” “Pretest to identify confusing questions.” “Provide anonymity to respondents.”Slides 57, 59

11 · Document analysis

What it is. Studying existing documents — forms, reports, memos, policy manuals, training manuals, org charts, regulations, user interfaces — for clues about the as-is system. Look for users' own forms and additions, and for unused forms and fields.

Why it matters

Cheapest technique, least training, and the best preparation before interviewing an expert. Reveals rules people forget to mention.

Example (slide 62). Vet clinic card: “Buffy” written in Name → label it Owner's Name. Staff added “Collie 7/6/99” → add animal type and date of birth. Phone lacks an area code → make it explicit.

Exam trapDocument analysis is not “read the requirements document”. It is as-is only, documents may be outdated, and it cannot capture tacit workarounds.

Slide 61
“Provides clues about existing ‘as-is’ system.” “Look for user's own forms and additions to forms.” “Look for unused forms and unused form elements.”Slide 61

12 · Observation

What it is. “Observation of work environment and work patterns.” Used because users often do not remember everything they do, and because it validates information gathered another way. Two warnings: behaviour changes when people are watched, and do not ignore periodic activities (weekly, monthly, annual).

Why it matters

It surfaces tacit knowledge and workarounds that interviews miss, and it is the recommended cure for unarticulated needs.

Example. A ward clerk writes discharge bed status on a whiteboard, phones admissions, then enters it at shift end. The requirement becomes a real-time update at discharge plus an interface.

Exam trapIgnoring the Hawthorne effect, and observing only one day so periodic work is missed. Observation is as-is only and gives no “why”.

Slide 64
“Observation of work environment and work patterns.” “Users/managers often don't remember everything they do.” “Behaviors change when people are watched.”Slide 64

13 · Scenarios

What it is. “Examples of interaction sessions which are concerned with a single type of interaction between an end-user and the system” — stories of how the system is used, mainly for adding detail to an outline requirements description. Five required parts: state before · normal flow · exceptions · concurrent activities · state after.

Why it matters

Exceptions and concurrent activity are exactly where hospital systems fail, and clinicians can validate a story.

Example. Book follow-up: pre-state opened referral; normal flow select → view → book → confirm; exception slot taken mid-booking; concurrent doctor cancels availability; post-state appointment recorded, reminder scheduled, registration updated.

Exam trapOnly writing the happy path. A scenario is not a use case diagram; it feeds use-case descriptions.

Slides 66–67
“Scenarios can be thought as stories which explain how the system is used. They are primarily useful for adding detail to an outline requirements description.”Slide 66

14 · User stories

What it is. “A short, simple description of a feature from the perspective of the person who desires the new functionality.” Form: As a <role>, I want <action> so that <benefit>. They go into a product backlog, prioritised by importance, risk and effort; each gets a task list, and a long task list means split the story. Before implementation, the customer writes an acceptance procedure. Properties: very low tech, high touch, easily updatable, portable. Hierarchy: theme → epic → story → task; product → release → sprint → daily.

Why it matters

Keeps requirements in the user's language, small enough to prioritise and estimate, and connected to acceptance testing.

Example. “As a secretary, I want to schedule appointments for our patients so that we can meet our patients' needs.” Non-functional story: print the daily schedule on the shared ward printer.

Exam trapA story is not a use case. The “so that” is the value, not a restatement. Acceptance criteria are written by the customer, and stories can express NFRs.

Slides 70–71
“Before a user story is implemented, an appropriate acceptance procedure must be written by the customer to determine whether the goals of the user story have been fulfilled.”Slide 70

15 · The other techniques

What it is. Simulations, prototyping, modelling · role-playing CRC cards with use cases · mind/concept mapping · organisational analysis (SWOT, product portfolio) · competitive system assessment · market analysis · data mining.

Why it matters

They cover what the six major techniques do not: novel interfaces, messy domain knowledge, strategic context, large existing datasets.

Example. A clickable appointment screen lets patients react to flow before any code; a mind map of “appointment, referral, registration, discharge, reminder” exposes missing links.

Exam trapPresenting these as equal to interviews/JAD. The deck calls them “other techniques”; the six major ones are interviews, JAD, questionnaires, document analysis, observation and scenarios (slide 77).

16 · Selecting and combining techniques

What it is. Seven criteria: type of information (as-is / improvements / to-be), depth, breadth, integration, user involvement, cost, and combining techniques. No single technique is sufficient; documents and observation require the least training; JAD is the most challenging.

Why it matters

Technique choice tests judgement, not memory. The matrix gives a defensible justification.

Example. Documents → interviews → observation → JAD → patient questionnaire → scenarios → user stories. Each covers another's weakness.

Exam trap“Use all techniques” is not an answer. Justify each by criterion, and remember document analysis/observation are as-is only.

Concepts

Open a card's slide definition when you need the exact wording for an exam.

1 · What analysis and negotiation are for

What it is. The process that establishes agreed requirements conforming to the characteristics — complete, unambiguous, consistent. It is closely linked to elicitation and deliberately hunts five faults: missing, conflicting, ambiguous, overlapping and unrealistic requirements. Cleland-Huang groups the work into conceptual modelling, allocating requirements to architectural components, and identifying conflicts that only appear once an implementation is considered.

Why it matters

A draft can be grammatically perfect and still contradict itself. Analysis is the last cheap moment to find that out.

Example. Reception wants 10-minute appointment slots to fit more patients; the doctor says an appointment needs 15 minutes. Neither sentence is vague — they conflict, and no amount of interviewing removes the conflict. Negotiation does.

Exam trapAnalysis is not a second round of elicitation. You are not collecting new needs; you are checking, elaborating and agreeing the ones you have.

Slides 7–9
“Objective of req. analysis and negotiation — To establish an agreed of requirements which conform to requirements characteristic e.g., complete, unambiguous, and consistent.” “This phase help developer to find missing req., req. conflict, ambiguous req., overlapping req., unrealistic req.”Slides 7, 9

2 · Five objectives

What it is. (1) Discover and clarify — turn vague needs into tangible requirements with one shared understanding. (2) Evaluate feasibility — achievable within budget, technology and time. (3) Resolve conflicts — negotiate a mutual agreement between stakeholders. (4) Define project scope — separate “must do” from “out of scope” to prevent creep. (5) Map system architecture — assign each validated requirement to the component that will satisfy it.

Why it matters

Each objective answers a different question: what, can we, who wins, how far, and where does it live.

Example (appointment system). Clarify “reminder” → push, then SMS after five minutes. Feasibility → 100% no-show prediction is impossible. Conflict → 10 vs 15 minutes. Scope → billing is out. Architecture → the reminder belongs to the notification component.

Exam trap“Resolve conflicts” is not “ask the senior manager to decide”. It means the disagreement is discussed and the trade-off recorded.

Slide 10
“Resolve Conflicts: Identify conflicting requirements between different stakeholders… and negotiate a mutual agreement.” “Define Project Scope: Clearly distinguish what the system ‘must do’ from what is ‘out of scope’ to effectively prevent scope creep.”Slide 10

3 · Four expected results

What it is. (1) The SRS — the formal agreement between client and team. (2) Analysis models — use case, class, activity, data-flow and entity-relationship diagrams. (3) A prioritised requirements list — what ships first and what waits. (4) A traceability matrix and architecture diagram — which component satisfies which requirement.

Why it matters

Four results for four readers: the client signs the SRS, designers read the models, the project manager reads the priorities, testers read the trace.

Example. For the clinic: SRS; a use case diagram of booking, cancelling and reminding; priorities Must/Should/Could; a matrix mapping each requirement to the booking, notification or records component.

Exam trapA diagram is a model, not a requirement. The SRS stays the agreement; the models are how you check and explain it.

Slide 11
“Software Requirements Specification (SRS): A formal, systematic document capturing all requirements to serve as the official agreement between the client and the development team.”Slide 11

4 · The process: three checks, then negotiation

What it is. Analysis runs three checks in order: necessity, consistency and completeness, feasibility. Each has a named output — unnecessary, incomplete / conflicting, infeasible. Everything that fails goes to negotiation, which has three outputs: discussion, prioritization, agreement. If agreement is not reached, the requirement returns to the checks.

Why it matters

The exam asks you to name the fault before proposing the fix. “It is incomplete” is a diagnosis; “we will interview more people” is not.

Example. Unnecessary — a 3D avatar for weekly quizzes. Incomplete — a retention rule left TBD. Conflicting — 10 vs 15-minute slots. Infeasible — 100% accurate live translation with zero delay.

Exam trapThe checks are ordered. You cannot judge feasibility before the requirement is known to be necessary and complete.

Slide 13
“Requirements analysis — Necessity checking · Consistency and completeness checking · Feasibility checking.” “Requirements negotiation — Requirements discussion · Requirements prioritization · Requirements agreement.”Slide 13

5 · Three ways to structure knowledge exam

What it is (Davis). (1) Partitioning — break a whole into structural parts. An artifact profile becomes unique ID, title, artist, creation date, medium, description, 3D model file and physical location. (2) Abstraction — group specifics under one general concept. “Museum exhibit” covers oil paintings, marble sculptures, textiles, coins and digital installations. (3) Projection — organise by viewpoint. Curators, visitors, educators, IT administrators and the collections database each see a different system.

Why it matters

The museum example is the slide's own; reuse it. The three names are easy marks and easy to confuse.

Example. Partitioning finds that the artifact record has no origin field. Abstraction finds that the system must handle coins as well as paintings. Projection finds that IT needs a bandwidth requirement no curator would mention.

Exam trapPartition = parts, abstraction = kinds, projection = views. A viewpoint is not a stakeholder class — “collections database” is a system view, not a person.

Slide 14
“1. Partitioning (Aggregation/Whole-Part Relationship)… 2. Abstraction (General/Specific Relationships)… 3. Projection (Multiple Perspectives/Viewpoints).”Slide 14

6 · Six classification dimensions

What it is (SWEBOK). (1) Functional or non-functional. (2) Origin — derived from higher-level requirements, an emergent property of components working together, or imposed directly by a stakeholder or other source. (3) Product or process — the process ones constrain contractor choice, process or standards. (4) Priority — mandatory, highly desirable, desirable, optional, balanced against cost. (5) Scope — global or narrow. (6) Volatility — stable or likely to change.

Why it matters

Classification decides who writes it, how it is tested and which component owns it.

Example. “Send a reminder 24 h before”: functional · derived · product · mandatory · narrow · stable. “PWA, responsive, WCAG 2.1 AA”: non-functional · imposed · product · mandatory · global · stable. “Egypt scavenger hunt for three months”: functional · imposed · product · desirable · narrow · volatile.

Exam trapEmergent is not the same as derived: an ATM's deposit, withdraw and transfer emerge from components working together. Priority is not “how much we like it”; it is balanced against development cost.

Slides 15–20
“Requirements can be classified on a number of dimensions… functional or nonfunctional… derived… or emergent property… imposed directly… product or the process… priority… scope… volatility/stability.”Slides 15–18

7 · Global and narrow scope

What it is. Scope is how far a requirement reaches. A global requirement cannot be allocated to one discrete component; it shapes the architecture and many components. A narrow requirement applies to one function or screen, leaves design choices open and has little effect on other requirements. The slide's example: “the system shall be a mobile application that connects to the exercise-monitor-embedded clock” is global; “the clock keeps the exercise data” and “transfer it to the app on request, in batch” are narrow.

Why it matters

Global requirements must be decided early, because they constrain every later choice. Narrow ones can be deferred without re-architecting.

Example (museum). Global: “a responsive PWA across desktop, tablet and phone, conforming to WCAG 2.1 AA” — it dictates the framework and every interface. Narrow: “a search bar on the gallery home page filtering by era”; “save a favourite's ID to the session”.

Exam trap“Narrow” does not mean unimportant, and “global” is not a synonym for non-functional — a global functional requirement exists too.

Slides 16–17
“Some requirements, particularly certain nonfunctional ones, have a global scope in that their satisfaction cannot be allocated to a discrete component.”Slide 16

8 · The analysis checklist exam

What it is. A short list of questions asked of each requirement or set. Eight items on the slide: conformance with business goals · premature design · combined requirements · unnecessary requirements · use of non-standard hardware/software · ambiguity · realism · testability. A checklist is a memory aid — it stops you forgetting a check — and should not exceed ten items.

Why it matters

The slide's own examples give you a bank of corrections to reuse: ads vs a dignified experience; JavaScript arrays as premature design; four functions in one sentence; a 3D gown as gold plating.

Example (slide 27). “An automated advertisement engine that displays pop-up banner commercials over the 3D gallery” fails conformance with the museum's stated goal of a dignified, educational experience. Corrected: sponsor acknowledgements in a dedicated section reached from the footer.

Exam trapThe eight checks are not the nine characteristics. Slide 32's bad requirement fails three at once: ambiguous (“if it is tested, can it be loaded from unknown sources?”), untestable (stated negatively) and untraceable (no identifier).

Slides 24–32
“A list of questions which the analyst may use to assess each requirement/set of requirements… Checklists should not normally include more than 10 items.”Slide 24

9 · Interaction matrices exam

What it is. Every requirement is compared with every other in a square matrix. Fill each cell: 1 for requirements that conflict, 1000 for requirements that overlap, 0 for independent. The matrix exposes interactions that a flat list hides: two requirements may ask for the same data, or set incompatible limits.

Why it matters

It converts “these requirements feel related” into a table you can act on: negotiate the 1s, merge the 1000s, leave the 0s.

Example. “Doctors set their own availability” (R1) conflicts with “the manager produces the schedule” (R5) if the manager must override a doctor's block. The matrix makes you notice before the testers do.

Exam trapThe codes are labels, not sizes: 1 is a conflict and 1000 is an overlap. Do not read 1000 as “a worse conflict”. Overlaps need merging; conflicts need negotiation.

Slides 33–34
“For requirements which conflict, fill in a 1. For requirements which overlap, fill in a 1000. For requirements which are independent, fill in a 0.”Slide 33

10 · Conceptual modelling and choosing a notation

What it is. Models of the real-world problem, built to understand the situation in which it occurs (as-is) and to depict a solution (to-be). They describe entities of the problem domain and their relationships. The notation is chosen on three factors: the nature of the problem (real-time software leans on state and parametric models, usually SysML; information systems lean on object and activity models, usually UML), the engineer's expertise, and the customer's process requirements — which may impose a notation or forbid one.

Why it matters

A model is where a misunderstanding becomes visible. Words hide it; a box with the wrong arrow does not.

Example. The clinic as-is is drawn as an activity diagram: patient arrives, reception searches paper diary, doctor writes the slot by hand. The to-be is a use case diagram of book, change, cancel and remind. The gap between the two pictures is the requirement work.

Exam trapNotation choice is a decision with three named reasons. “We always use UML” covers factor 2 only, and factor 3 can override it.

Slides 35–36
“Their purpose is to aid in understanding the situation in which the problem occurs (as-is system), as well as depicting a solution (to-be system).”Slide 35

11 · Functional, structural and behavioural models

What it is. Each model answers one question. Use case diagram — what capabilities or services the system offers and to which actors. Class diagram — which objects collaborate to serve those use cases, and their relationships. Activity diagram — the flow of a business process. Sequence and state-machine diagrams — behaviour over time. DFD and ERD — data movement and data structure. Component and three-tier diagrams — the architecture that will hold it all.

Why it matters

Exams ask “which model would you use to show X?”. Answer with the question the model answers, not its name alone.

Example (clinic). Use case: “Schedule appointment”, actors client and medical staff. Class: Appointment, Patient, Doctor, Slot. Activity: the booking flow including the referral branch. Three-tier: interface, application logic, data.

Exam trapA use case diagram is not a requirements list — it shows capabilities, not conditions or constraints. And one diagram is never the whole analysis.

Slides 37–40, 44–45, 49–50
“Use case diagram: specify system capabilities or system services.” “Class diagram: the collaboration objects to serve the defined use case.”Slides 37–38

12 · Requirements allocation

What it is. Deriving the solution architecture during analysis and assigning each requirement to the component responsible for satisfying it. Once a set is allocated, each component can be analysed further to discover the requirements it needs in order to interact with the others. The slide's search-engine example: the query module accepts keywords; the ranking module sorts by relevance score; the indexing module updates the index in batch every 24 hours; the crawler controller gives each crawler a predefined list of sites.

Why it matters

It proves coverage — every requirement has an owner — and it discovers interface requirements no stakeholder would volunteer.

Example. Allocating “remind 24 h before” to the notification component forces two new requirements: the booking component must publish appointment changes, and the patient-record component must supply a current phone number.

Exam trapAllocation is not detailed design. The output is responsibility and interface, not class names, databases or algorithms.

Slides 46–48
“This is requirements allocation — the assignment to architecture components responsible for satisfying the requirements.”Slide 46

13 · Formal analysis

What it is. Expressing requirements in a language with formally defined semantics. It matters most for high-integrity systems. Two benefits: the language states requirements precisely and unambiguously, and the requirements can be reasoned over, so desired properties can be proved. The slide's map: sequential languages — Larch, OBJ (algebraic) and Z, VDM, B (model-based); concurrent languages — Lotos (algebraic) and CSP, Petri nets (model-based).

Why it matters

It changes the question from “do we agree this sentence is clear?” to “can we prove this property holds?”.

Example. An infusion pump's interlock — “the pump shall never deliver while the line is disconnected” — can be modelled and checked, rather than argued about in prose.

Exam trapFormal is not “more detailed prose”. Without formal semantics you cannot prove anything; the benefit is proof, not length.

Slides 51–52
“The formal expression of requirements requires a language with formally defined semantics… requirements can be reasoned over, permitting desired properties of the specified software to be proven.”Slide 51

14 · Requirements negotiation

What it is. The process of discussing conflicts and finding a compromise all stakeholders can live with. Conflicts arise between two stakeholders wanting incompatible features, between requirements and resources, or between functional and non-functional requirements. Every final requirement is a compromise governed by organisational needs, individual stakeholder needs, design and implementation constraints, and budget and schedule. Meetings are the most effective method; they run in three stages — information (explain the problem), discussion (how to resolve it), resolution (agree actions) — and are chaired by someone who is not a stakeholder.

Why it matters

An unnegotiated conflict does not disappear; it returns as a change request after design, when it costs ten times more.

Example. Doctor 15 minutes, reception 10. The meeting hears both (information), proposes 12 minutes plus one overbooked slot per hour (discussion), and records who accepted what (resolution).

Exam trapThe chair must be neutral. And a resolution records the trade-off — what each side gave up — otherwise the same conflict returns.

Slides 53–55
“Requirements negotiation is the process of discussing the conflicts in requirements and finding some compromise which all of the stakeholders can live with.” “An information stage… A discussion stage… A resolution stage.”Slides 53–55

15 · Prioritisation: cost/benefit and AHP exam

What it is. Two approaches. Cost/benefit analysis scores each requirement on the value of implementing it against the penalty of omitting it, on a 1–5 scale. AHP (Analytic Hierarchy Process) compares every unique pair of requirements and decides which is higher priority, and by how much. The slide's bank example: biometric login is value 5, penalty 2; the compliance update is value 1, penalty 5.

Why it matters

The exam wants the rule you used, not only the winner. Say “penalty of omission” out loud.

Example. Requirement A is a value-driven feature: users stay happy. Requirement B is risk-driven: a missed legal deadline brings fines, app-store suspension and reputational damage. B ships first even though A scores higher on value.

Exam trapSlide 60 prints A = 7 and B = 6 yet orders B first. The sum is not the rule: an extreme penalty dominates a convenience benefit. State that explicitly rather than repeating the arithmetic as if it agreed.

Slides 57–60
“Value of implementation (benefit) versus penalty of omission (risk)… Requirement B: Must Do First: The extreme penalty makes this an absolute mandatory priority.”Slides 58–60

16 · Three analysis approaches exam

What it is. The basic analysis process has three steps: understand the as-is, identify improvements, develop to-be requirements. The three approaches differ in how much they change. BPA — automate some work, least change, low risk and low payoff, most time spent on the as-is. BPI — moderate change to improve the efficiency of the as-is or copy a competitor. BPR — a complete makeover, focused on the to-be, little time on the as-is, high risk and high payoff.

Why it matters

The approach sets how much as-is analysis you do and how boldly you may redraw the process.

Example (hospital). BPA: replace the paper booking form with an e-form. BPI: redesign the queue so registration and triage share one flow. BPR: replace walk-in care with telemedicine-first triage and home delivery.

Exam trapBPR is not “BPA with extra features”. It questions whether the process should exist, and deliberately spends little time on the as-is.

Slides 66–68
“Business Process Reengineering (BPR) — Most amount of changes… a complete makeover. Focus is on the to-be system; little time spent on the as-is system.”Slide 67

17 · Seven analysis strategies exam

What it is. (1) Problem analysis — users and managers name problems and their solutions; small, incremental improvements. (2) Root-cause analysis — users give prioritised problems and all possible causes; the analyst finds solutions and common causes. (3) Duration analysis — time per step vs total time → integrate or parallelise. (4) Activity-based costing — cost per step, direct and indirect, to find the costliest step. (5) Informal benchmarking — study how others do the process, from reviews and customers up to formal partnerships. (6) Outcome analysis — start from the customer's desired outcome and extend the process. (7) Technology analysis — list interesting technologies and how each could be applied.

Why it matters

Strategies are how you find improvements; Lesson 3's techniques are how you gather requirements. Keep the two lists apart.

Example. Stock-outs → root-cause tree. A long wait between steps → duration analysis. An insurance claim that ends in a repaired car → outcome analysis. A loan process full of manual entry → technology analysis (RPA).

Exam trapRoot-cause analysis does not ask users for solutions, and “problem analysis” does. Mixing the two loses the mark for the definition.

Slides 69–74
“Root Cause Analysis — Users are not asked for solutions, but for: a list of (prioritized) problems; all possible root causes for those problems.”Slide 69

18 · Four common mistakes

What it is. (1) Reducing analysis time to meet a deadline — the team builds the wrong product because it never understood the problem. (2) Requirements gold plating — stakeholders keep adding features outside the agreed scope; the project bloats, then misses deadline and budget. (3) Developer gold plating — engineers add unrequested technical features because they are interesting to build. (4) Lack of user involvement — the system works technically but is rejected because it does not fit real workflows.

Why it matters

All four trade a small saving now for a large cost later — the same shape as the defect-cost curve in Lesson 1.

Example. A three-week “head start” that skips observation produces a booking screen with no referral branch; the branch is added in testing, when it costs far more.

Exam trapGold plating has two sources — stakeholders and developers. Name which one you mean; the cures differ (scope control vs design review).

Slides 75–76
“Reducing Analysis Time… The project team builds the wrong product because they did not take the time to fully understand the real business problem.”Slide 75

Checklist on a lab-portal requirement

Before: “The system shall let patients view results on a dedicated retinal-display kiosk in the lobby, and shall also predict the diagnosis from the result.” Non-standard hardware — a bespoke kiosk, unsupported and unfundable. Unnecessary — diagnosis prediction is gold plating: it is not what a results portal is for. Realism — a prediction with no accountable clinician is not deliverable in a regulated setting. Conformance with business goals — the stated goal is access to released results, not interpretation. After: “A patient shall view each released result in the portal within one minute of release.” The kiosk becomes a design question; the prediction becomes a separate proposal, if anyone asks for it.

BPA, BPI or BPR for pharmacy dispensing

The problem is a queue: screening and packing happen at the same bench, so a pharmacist stands idle while the other station is overloaded. Choose BPI. The process keeps its steps and its controls; you merge two adjacent stations and re-sequence them, which is a moderate change with a measurable queue-time target. BPA would only print the label faster and leave the queue. BPR would replace pharmacist dispensing with automated cabinets and reserve the pharmacist for high-risk drugs — a different process, high risk, and not justified by a queue alone. State the condition: if the queue target is missed after BPI, BPR becomes the next question.

Where the three models sit

One requirements definition, three functional views.

The deck's three representations: diagram, description, activity. A use case that exists only as an ellipse is a name, not a specification. สามตัวแทนของบท: แผนภาพ คำอธิบาย และ activity diagram use case ที่มีแค่ ellipse คือชื่อ ยังไม่ใช่ข้อกำหนด

Concepts

Draw the shape, then write the words; the exam can ask for either.

1 · Three representations, one model

What it is. Functional requirements become three representations: a use case diagram (a bird's-eye view of the system's functionality), a use case description for each use case (the flows in words), and an activity diagram that augments the use case with the business process. The models are logical — independent of whether the work is manual or computerised.

Why it matters

Each view answers a different question: what can be done, what happens in one case, and in what order across roles.

Example. For an appointment system: the diagram shows “Book appointment” and “Remind patient”; the description numbers the steps of booking; the activity diagram shows reception, doctor and patient lanes with the reschedule decision.

Exam trapThese are not three alternative deliverables. A use case without a description, or a description without a process view, is incomplete — the deck adds the activity diagram to augment each use case.

Slides 3, 5, 42
“Develop use cases from the requirements… Develop a use case description to document each use-case… Develop an activity diagram to augment each use-case.”Slide 3

2 · Anatomy of a use case diagram

What it is. Five ingredients: actors and actor relationships, use cases and use case relationships, the interaction (association) between actor and use case, the system boundary, and the system name. Actors sit outside the boundary; use cases sit inside it.

Why it matters

The boundary is a scope decision, not decoration: anything outside it is another system's job.

Example. The SMS gateway is an actor, not a use case; sending the message is inside the boundary, the network that carries it is outside.

Exam trapAn actor is a role, not a named person, and it can be a machine, a sensor, another system or an organisational unit.

Slides 6–7
“Use Case ประกอบด้วย 1. ผู้แสดง (Actor) and actor relationship 2. กรณีใช้งาน (Use Case) and use case relationship 3. การโต้ตอบ (Interaction) between actor and use case 4. System Boundary 5. System Name.”Slide 6

3 · Finding actors, and four types

What it is. Ask who or what produces or consumes the system's data: who uses the main functions, who needs support, who maintains it, which devices it handles, which systems it talks to, who cares about the result. Actors come in five kinds — human, machine, external system, organisational unit, sensor — and four roles: primary business (benefits), primary system (initiates), external server (responds), external receiver (receives value).

Why it matters

Missing an actor is missing a requirement; naming a person instead of a role makes the model brittle.

Example. In the clinic: patient (primary business), receptionist (primary system), SMS gateway (external server), pharmacy printer (external receiver).

Exam trap“Doctor” is a role, but “Dr Somchai” is not an actor. A sensor or a legacy system can be an actor with no human at all.

Slides 19–20
“Primary business actor… the stakeholder that primarily benefits from the execution of the use case.” “External server actor… responds to a request from the use case.”Slide 20

4 · Associations, multiplicity, generalisation

What it is. An association connects an actor to a use case. Multiplicity is the number of associations: no arrowhead means information flows both ways, a solid arrowhead means one way. Actor generalisation means instances of the specialised actor can substitute for the general one — a nurse inherits what a staff member can do.

Why it matters

The arrow shows information direction, not who starts the use case; initiation is recorded by the primary actor in the description.

Example. Patient → “Book appointment” is two-way (the system returns a slot); “Remind patient” → SMS gateway is one-way (the system sends, the gateway does not answer with data).

Exam trapAn arrowhead is not an initiation arrow. And generalisation between actors is not the same as generalisation between use cases (clause 6).

Slides 8–9, 21
“Multiplicity = number of associations, No arrowhead = two-way flow of information, Solid arrowhead = one-way flow of information.”Slide 9

5 · Finding use cases, and the six-step procedure

What it is. For each actor ask: which functions does the actor require, what must it read/create/modify/store, what events must it be notified about or notify, and what new function would simplify its work. Then follow the deck's procedure: review the requirements definition; identify the boundary, primary actors and goals, business processes and major use cases; review the set and split or combine to the right size; add missing cases; place and draw actors and use cases; draw the boundary; add associations.

Why it matters

Sizing is the skill: too fine and the diagram becomes a function list; too coarse and one ellipse hides three different goals.

Example. “Manage appointment” is too coarse; split into “Book”, “Reschedule”, “Cancel”, and keep “Remind patient” as a separate system-triggered case.

Exam trapThe six steps are a sequence with a review loop — “carefully review the current set” comes before drawing, not after.

Slides 10, 22
“Review the requirements definition… Carefully review the current set of use cases — Split or combine some to create the right size.”Slide 10

6 · Include, extend, generalisation exam

What it is. Include: the base use case incorporates the included behaviour, which is a required part of the larger case — used for common behaviour (both “Update grade” and “Generate Grade Report” include “Verify student ID”). Extend: the base is complete on its own; the extension adds optional behaviour and points at the base. Generalisation: the specialised use case inherits the actors, behaviour and meaning of the general one, and may add or override it.

Why it matters

The three arrows are the most common five-mark question in this deck; direction and optionality are the marks.

Example. “Book appointment” includes “Verify patient identity” (always); “Send reminder” extends “Book appointment” (only when the clinic opts into reminders); “Book online” and “Book at counter” generalise “Book appointment”.

Exam trapInclude is required and the arrow points to the included case; extend is optional and the arrow points from the extension to the base. Reversing either loses the mark.

Slides 23–25
“The base use case incorporates the behavior of the included use case… The extension use case represents additional functionality that is optional.”Slides 24–25

7 · Cockburn's levels: cloud to clam

What it is. Three bands: summary — cloud (enterprise: advertise goods, sell goods, manage inventory) and kite (department overview: register students); user goal — sea level, the level users care about, one business activity of roughly 2–20 minutes (book an appointment, check out an order); subfunction — fish (detail: log in, choose a class) and clam (most detailed: validate login).

Why it matters

Level decides the size of a use case and whether a step belongs in the description or in an included subfunction.

Example. “Register a continuing student” is sea; “pay academic fees” is fish; “validate login” is clam; “register students” for the whole faculty is kite.

Exam trapThe colours and animal names are Cockburn's; the exam expects the three bands and one example each, not the colour names alone.

Slides 16–18
“Sea level (Blue): This often has the greatest interest for users… each person should be able to do one blue level activity in anywhere from 2 to 20 minutes.”Slide 17

8 · The description's fields

What it is. A use case description records: name (a verb–noun phrase), ID (for tracing design decisions back to a requirement), type (overview or detail; essential or real), primary actor (the one that starts execution), brief description, importance (for release planning), stakeholders, and triggers — external (a customer orders) or temporal (a book is overdue).

Why it matters

The ID is what lets you trace a design decision back to a requirement; without it the description is an orphan.

Example. A reminder use case has a temporal trigger (24 hours before the appointment), not an actor clicking anything.

Exam trapA trigger is not an actor: temporal triggers exist, and the primary actor is the one that starts execution, not the one who benefits.

Slide 29
“Name: verb-noun phrase… ID number: for identification and tracing design decisions back to a requirement… Trigger(s): event(s) that cause the use case to begin — External… Temporal.”Slide 29

9 · Relationships and flows

What it is. The relationships field records association, extend, include and generalisation. The flow of events has three parts: the normal flow (usual activities), sub-flows (decomposed normal steps that simplify the case), and alternate or exceptional flows (the non-normal paths). The deck groups the last two as one kind; splitting alternate from exceptional is how the marks are earned. Optional characteristics: complexity, time to execute, precondition.

Why it matters

Examiners deduct for a description with only a happy path; the alternate flow is where the requirements actually live.

Example. Normal: patient chooses a slot, system reserves it. Alternate: the slot was taken while the patient decided — offer the next three slots. Exceptional: the scheduling service is down — record the request for callback.

Exam trapAlternate is not the same as exceptional: alternate is a legitimate different path, exceptional is a failure or blocked condition. Both must be written.

Slide 30
“Normal flow: the usual set of activities… Sub-flows… Alternate or exceptional flows: those not considered the norm.”Slide 30

10 · Essential vs real, overview vs detail

What it is. Two independent dimensions. Essential describes only implementation-independent issues; real commits to technology, design and UI. Overview documents only the overview and relationships; detail documents all parts including the flow based on the activity diagram.

Why it matters

Writing “Google Calendar” in an essential description silently makes a design decision nobody negotiated.

Example. Slide 31's pair, both at detail level: essential — “receptionist matches the desired appointment time with the available time”; real — “receptionist looks up available dates on Google Calendar”.

Exam trapThe two axes are independent: a real overview exists, and so does an essential detail. Do not collapse them into one pair of opposites.

Slide 31
“Overview… documenting only the overview and relationships parts… Detail… documenting all parts, including the flow of events based on the activity diagram.”Slide 31

11 · Writing the normal flow exam

What it is. Each step is a subject–verb–direct object–preposition–indirect object sentence (SVDPI). Make the initiator and receiver explicit, write from an independent observer's perspective, and keep the set to four parts: the primary actor requests; the system validates; the system processes and may change state; the system returns the result. Repeating instructions go after the set; steps should be about the same size (KISS).

Why it matters

The sentence pattern is marked mechanically: a step without a subject or a receiver loses the point even if the behaviour is right.

Example. Bad: “Appointment is booked.” Good: “The patient selects a time slot; the system reserves the slot and sends the patient a confirmation.”

Exam trapDo not write from the actor's voice (“I choose a slot”) or the system's voice; the description is written by an independent observer.

Slide 32
“Write each step in the form of subject-verb-direct object, and optionally, preposition-indirect object (SVDPI sentence)… Write from independent observer's perspective.”Slide 32

12 · The description process

What it is. Choose a high-priority use case and write the overview and relationships parts; describe the normal flow in SVDPI steps; identify and write alternate and exceptional flows; review with the user for correctness; look for include, extend and generalisation opportunities and add or merge cases; repeat until the major use cases are documented.

Why it matters

The review with the user is a step, not a courtesy: it is where wrong actors and missing alternate flows are caught.

Example. After reviewing booking with reception, the team discovers the “patient is already registered twice” exceptional flow and adds it.

Exam trapThe process starts from a priority, not from the first use case alphabetically; writing everything at full detail wastes the effort.

Slides 32–33
“Review the use case description with the user to confirm that it is correct… Repeat until a sufficient number of major use cases are documented.”Slide 33

13 · What an activity diagram is for

What it is. A business process model that describes the activities supporting a process and depicts their sequence. It gives a more detailed graphical view than the use case; diagrams are abstract and model behaviour independent of objects. It can model a high-level workflow across many use cases, the detail of a single use case, or the detail of one method.

Why it matters

It is the communication tool between analyst and user: a wrong decision or a missing parallel step is visible in one picture.

Example. The appointment process diagram shows reception booking, the doctor confirming, and the reminder being sent — three use cases in one flow.

Exam trapAn activity diagram is not a screen flow: it models the business process and its decisions, not navigation between pages.

Slides 41, 44
“Activity diagrams depict the sequence of these activities… They model behavior independent of objects.”Slide 44

14 · The symbols

What it is. A filled circle is the initial node; a rounded rectangle is an action — one step that cannot be decomposed, labelled by its name; an activity is a set of actions and is named with a verb + noun; a rectangle is an object node, labelled by its class name, and a dashed arrow is an object flow while a solid arrow is a control flow; a diamond is a decision (with guard conditions) or a merge; a thick bar is a fork (parallel paths) or a join (paths meet again); a bullseye is the final-activity node and a circle with an X is the final-flow node, which stops one path while the others continue; swimlanes group activities by the party responsible.

Why it matters

Most marks are lost on a fork with no join, or a decision drawn as a merge.

Example. After booking, the system does two things in parallel: send the confirmation and update the doctor's calendar. That is a fork, then a join before the final node.

Exam trapA diamond is not automatically a decision: it is a merge when several paths come in and one goes out, with no guard.

Slides 45, 48–49
“There should be one node at the top or top left… Mostly there should be one node at the bottom or bottom right… After a fork, there is a join.”Slides 48–49

15 · Swimlanes and the drawing procedure

What it is. Swimlanes show a high-level process across business units; the most important individual or object goes at the top or left. Use decision/merge for alternatives and fork/join for parallel work, and add the input and output of each activity as an object flow. The procedure: choose the process; review requirements, the use case diagram and other documentation; identify activities and connect them with control flows, object flows and nodes; identify decisions and parallelism; draw, minimising crossings and laying out left-to-right or top-to-bottom.

Why it matters

Lane assignment is where “who does what” becomes visible — and where a missing actor shows up as an activity with no lane.

Example. If “update doctor calendar” has no lane, the diagram has just revealed a missing responsibility in the requirements.

Exam trapThe procedure's last two rules are marks: minimise crossing lines, and lay the diagram left-to-right and/or top-to-bottom.

Slides 46–50
“Identify any decisions that must be made and potential parallelism… Minimize crossing lines… Lay out in left-to-right and/or top-to-bottom order.”Slide 50

16 · How the three models interlock

What it is. The functional requirements feed the use case diagram; each use case gets a description; an activity diagram augments the use case and may span several cases. The deck's Figure 8-2 (slide 56) is a composition, not a pipeline: Functional Models includes the use-case diagram, the use case descriptions and the activity diagram; use cases have scenarios, descriptions contain stakeholders, activities/actions and events, and the activity diagram contains object nodes, activities/actions and flows. This figure is the page's process view of the same three models — and each can reveal a gap in the others.

Why it matters

An exam question that gives you one view often asks what the other two would add; this figure is the answer.

Example. The activity diagram shows the reminder leaving the system; the description supplies the trigger and alternate flow; the diagram shows which actor owns each step.

Exam trapDo not present the three as sequential deliverables with nothing shared; they are checked against each other and against the requirements.

The [no] path goes to “eject card” and ends at a final-flow node — that path stops while the machine stays available; it does not need a join. The [yes] path crosses into the Bank host lane for “debit the account”, then returns to the ATM lane and enters a fork: dispense cash and print receipt run in parallel, and a join waits for both before the flow ends at the final-activity node. Debit comes before dispense — the order is a business rule, and the diagram must show it. The withdrawal record is an object node flowing between the ATM and the host.

1 · The structural model, and when it is drawn

What it is. Class diagrams and object diagrams describe the data of the problem domain. In analysis you draw a conceptual model — the logical organisation of objects, with no statement of how they are stored, created or manipulated. In design that model evolves into a technology-level design model. Each pass adds detail.

Why it matters

It is the model that answers “what data does this system keep?”, which the use case and activity diagrams never answer.

Example. The clinic's booking case produces Appointment, Patient, Slot and Reminder as candidate classes; where those rows live in a database is a design question, not an analysis one.

Exam trapDrawing database tables in the analysis model. A conceptual class has no foreign key and no column type.

Slide 7
“First in analysis, analysts draw a conceptual model which shows the logical organization of the objects without indicating how the objects are stored, created, or manipulated.”Slide 7

2 · Classes, attributes, operations

What it is. A class is a template for instances of people, places or things. An attribute is a property that describes the state of one instance. An operation is an action the class can perform.

Why it matters

The three words are the vocabulary of every class-diagram answer; mixing an attribute with an operation is the first mark lost.

Example. Patient: attributes patientID, name, dateOfBirth; operations bookAppointment(), cancelAppointment(). “bookAppointment” is behaviour, not data — it is not an attribute.

Exam trapPutting a verb in the attribute compartment (“books appointment”). If it acts, it is an operation.

Slide 9
“Classes — Templates for instances of people, places, or things. Attributes — Properties that describe the state of an instance of a class. Operations — Actions or functions that a class can perform.”Slide 9

3 · Concrete, abstract and domain classes

What it is. A concrete class is instantiated (Employee, Customer). An abstract class is an abstraction that never exists on its own (Person, generalising Employee and Customer). Classes are also classified by what they represent: domain classes are the analysis focus; the others are user-interface, data-structure, file-structure, operating-environment, document and multimedia classes.

Why it matters

It tells you what to draw at requirements time and what to leave to design.

Example. Doctor and Patient are domain classes; a “MainWindow” or a “PatientTable” class belongs to design, not analysis.

Exam trapTreating an abstract class as something you can create. Person is not an object; a Doctor is.

Slide 10
“Concrete class: used to create objects… Abstract class: useful abstraction, not actually exist in the real world.”Slide 10

4 · The class box and visibility

What it is. The class box has three compartments: name, attributes, operations. Visibility marks who can see a member: + public, − private, # protected. Trivial accessors (get/set) and constructors are normally omitted so the diagram shows only domain operations.

Why it matters

Visibility is information hiding made visible, and it is a cheap mark in a “draw the class diagram” question.

Example. Appointment: + appointmentID, + startTime, − status; + reschedule(), − validateSlot(). Status is hidden because only the class may change it.

Exam trapFilling the box with getters and setters. The deck says to omit them and keep domain-specific operations.

Slides 13–15
“Visibility indicates the level of information hiding… create or delete an instance and get or set attribute values are omitted.”Slides 13–15

5 · Associations, multiplicity, association classes

What it is. An association is a line between classes that need to know each other. Multiplicity states how many instances participate at each end (1, 0..1, 1..*, *). An association class holds data that belongs to the link itself, which is how a many-to-many relationship carries attributes. A verb phrase on the line names the relationship, a small solid triangle gives its reading direction, and + before a word on the line marks a role.

Why it matters

Multiplicity is where most diagram marks sit: the line alone says nothing about how many.

Example. Patient 1 — * Appointment: one patient has many appointments, each appointment belongs to one patient. The deck’s association classes: Illness * — * Symptom with Treatment on the link; Student * — * Course with Grade. In the clinic, Patient * — * Doctor is carried by Appointment, which holds the date, time and room that belong to the pair, not to either class.

Exam trapReading 1..* as “one or more patients” when it is written at the wrong end. Read each end as “how many of that class”.

Slides 18–19
“Multiplicity… Common in many-to-many relationships: association classes.”Slides 18–19

6 · Aggregation versus composition

What it is. Both are whole–part relationships. Aggregation (hollow diamond): the part can exist without the whole and can belong to another whole. Composition (filled diamond): the whole creates and destroys its parts, a part belongs to one whole only, and if the whole dies the part dies with it.

Why it matters

It is a lifecycle claim, not a strength claim — and the filled diamond is the one worth remembering.

Example. Ward ◇— Bed is aggregation (a bed survives the ward's closure); Appointment ◆— Slot is composition (the slot exists because the appointment holds it, and dies when it is cancelled).

Exam trapUsing the filled diamond whenever the relationship “feels strong”. Ask who destroys the part.

Slides 20–21
“The whole is responsible for the creation and destruction of its parts. If the whole were to die, the part would die with it.”Slides 20–21

7 · Generalisation and inheritance

What it is. A generalisation puts shared attributes and operations in a general class and lets specialised classes inherit them; the hollow triangle points at the general class. The specialised class may add or override members. The deck’s three relationship types in one line: generalisation is a-kind-of (Employee is a kind of Person), aggregation is a-part-of / has-parts (Employee is a part of Department), association is associated-with (Appointment is associated with Patient) — “usually a weaker form of aggregation”.

Why it matters

It is the difference between “is a kind of” and “has a” — the two questions that decide whether you draw a triangle or a diamond.

Example. Person generalises Patient and Doctor (both have name and ID); Patient adds insuranceNumber. Booking is not a kind of Appointment — it is related by association.

Exam trapConfusing generalisation with aggregation. “A doctor is a person” is a triangle; “a ward has beds” is a diamond.

Slides 11, 15
“Generalization… represents relationships that are ‘a-kind-of’. Aggregation… ‘a-part-of’ or ‘has-parts’. Association… miscellaneous relationships between classes, usually a weaker form of aggregation.”Slide 11

8 · Simplifying the diagram: views and packages

What it is. A fully populated class diagram is unreadable. The deck's simplifications: show only concrete classes; use a view (only the classes of one use case, only one kind of relationship, or a reduced level of detail per class); or group classes into packages.

Why it matters

The examiner marks a diagram that communicates, not one that lists every class in the system.

Example. For the “book appointment” question, draw the use-case view: Patient, Appointment, Slot and Reminder only — no billing classes, no audit classes.

Exam trapAnswering “more detail is better”. Detail without a view hides the relationship being asked about.

Slide 22
“Use case view: show only the classes and relationships relevant to a particular use case… Packages show logical groups of classes.”Slide 22

9 · Four ways to find the objects

What it is. The four common approaches: textual analysis of the use case descriptions, brainstorming with users and developers, a common object list of typical business categories (physical things, incidents, roles, interactions, places, containers), and patterns. Most analysts combine them. The deck adds one exercise after the list is made: role-playing the use cases — team members act the actors and objects step by step, make each vague step explicit, and discover the objects, attributes and operations the description missed.

Why it matters

An exam that hands you a use case description is testing the first; one that asks “how would you find classes?” wants all four.

Example. Textual analysis of “the patient books an appointment” gives Patient, Appointment and books() before any brainstorming meeting happens.

Exam trapListing only brainstorming because it is the easiest to describe. The deck names four.

Slide 23
“Most system analysts use a combination of the following four most common approaches to identify candidate objects/classes: textual analysis, brainstorming, common object list, pattern.”Slide 23

10 · Textual analysis: part of speech to model

What it is. Read the use case description and map grammar to model elements: proper noun → instance; common noun → class or attribute; collective noun → class made of another class's objects; doing verb → operation; being verb → inheritance; having verb → aggregation or composition; modal verb → constraint; adjective → attribute.

Why it matters

It turns a paragraph into a class list mechanically, which is exactly what a case-study question rewards.

Example. “The clinic assigns a doctor to each appointment” — clinic and appointment are classes, assigns is an operation, each signals multiplicity.

Exam trapTreating every noun as a class. A noun that describes a property (colour, status) is an attribute.

Slides 24–27
“Part of Speech — Proper noun → Instance; Common noun → Class (or attribute); Doing verb → Operation; Having verb → Aggregation/Composition.”Slides 24–27

11 · Patterns: analysis and entity-control-boundary

What it is. A pattern is a reusable arrangement of classes. Analysis patterns (e.g. the sale pattern) are adapted to the domain; the entity-control-boundary pattern separates what the system knows (entity), what it does for a use case (control) and what the actor talks to (boundary).

Why it matters

Patterns stop you inventing a structure per project, and ECB is what the textbook's own examples use.

Example. In the clinic model, Patient and Appointment are entities, BookAppointment is a control, and the booking screen is a boundary.

Exam trapCopying a pattern unchanged. The deck shows patterns being merged and adapted, not pasted.

Slides 30, 39
“Useful groupings of collaborating classes by experts which provide solutions to commonly occurring problems (they are reusable)… In the analysis phase, we will see an example of analysis patterns and entity-control-boundary pattern.”Slide 30

12 · Seven steps, then verify

What it is. The procedure: textual analysis on the use case descriptions → use the SVDPI flow sentences → identify classes → add attributes → add operations (responsibilities) → add relationships with collaborators → add visibility and multiplicity. Then review (remove what is unnecessary), incorporate patterns, and validate against the use-case diagrams and descriptions.

Why it matters

The last step is the one that earns the “why” marks: the three models must stay consistent.

Example. If the description says “system sends the patient a reminder” but the class diagram has no Reminder connection, one of the two models is wrong — the step-10 check finds it.

Exam trapStopping after step 7. Steps 8–10 are review, patterns and consistency.

Slides 46–48
“Textual analysis on use case descriptions… Review and validate the model—always keep use-case diagrams, use-case descriptions, and class diagram consistent.”Slides 46–48

เท็จ. ตรวจสอบได้และวัดได้เป็นเพียงสองในสี่ข้อของนิยาม — ยังต้อง (ข) จำเป็นต่อการแก้ปัญหาของผู้มีส่วนได้ส่วนเสีย, (ค) มีเงื่อนไขที่วัดได้และถูกจำกัดด้วย constraint และ (ง) กำหนดความสามารถของระบบ ไม่ใช่ความสามารถของผู้ใช้. คำตอบที่ได้คะแนนคือการไล่ครบสี่ข้อ ไม่ใช่แค่บอกว่า “ไม่พอ”.

T2“system requirements ครอบคลุม user requirements ความต้องการของผู้มีส่วนได้ส่วนเสียอื่น เช่น หน่วยงานกำกับ และความต้องการที่ไม่มีแหล่งที่มาเป็นบุคคล”

“System requirements encompass user requirements, the requirements of other stakeholders such as regulators, and requirements with no identifiable human source.”

2 marks · Lesson 2 · clause 3

จริง. นี่คือถ้อยคำของสไลด์ 8 — system requirements กว้างกว่า user requirements เพราะรวมผู้มีส่วนได้ส่วนเสียที่ไม่มีเสียงเดียว (หน่วยงานกำกับ กฎหมาย) และความต้องการที่ไม่มีแหล่งบุคคล. เหตุผลที่ต้องระบุคือความต่างจาก software requirements ซึ่งเป็นส่วนย่อยที่ได้มาจาก system requirements.

T3“primary actor คือผู้ที่เริ่มการทำงานของ use case และไม่จำเป็นต้องเป็นผู้ที่ได้รับประโยชน์จาก use case นั้น”

“The primary actor is the one that starts execution of the use case, and need not be the one who benefits from it.”

2 marks · Lesson 5 · clause 3 and clause 8

จริง. use case description นิยาม primary actor ว่าเป็นผู้เริ่มการทำงาน ส่วนผู้ที่ได้ประโยชน์คือ primary business actor ซึ่งอาจเป็นคนละคน. ตัวอย่าง: เจ้าหน้าที่ต้อนรับเริ่ม “จองนัด” แทนผู้ป่วย แต่ผู้ได้รับประโยชน์คือผู้ป่วย — เขียนสลับกันเมื่อไรเสียคะแนน.

T4“iterative คือการนำกระบวนการเดิมไปใช้ซ้ำกับระดับของระบบที่ต่ำลง”

“Iterative means applying the same process again at successively lower levels of the system.”

2 marks · Lesson 2 · clause 16

เท็จ. การทำซ้ำที่ระดับต่ำลงคือ recursive (เรียกซ้ำ) ส่วน iterative (ทำซ้ำ) คือการใช้กระบวนการเดิมซ้ำที่ระดับเดิม. ตัวแยกคือระดับ ไม่ใช่จำนวนรอบ — คำตอบที่ถูกต้องต้องเอ่ยทั้งสองคำและชี้ว่าประโยคสลับกัน.

T5“ใน interaction matrix เลข 1000 หมายถึงข้อขัดแย้งที่รุนแรงกว่าเลข 1”

“In an interaction matrix, 1000 marks a more severe conflict than 1.”

2 marks · Lesson 4 · clause 9

เท็จ. เลขเหล่านี้เป็นป้ายประเภท ไม่ใช่ขนาด: 1 = ขัดแย้ง (ต้องเจรจา), 1000 = ทับซ้อน (ต้องรวมเข้าด้วยกัน), 0 = ไม่เกี่ยวกัน. 1000 ไม่ได้รุนแรงกว่า — มันเป็นคนละปัญหาที่ต้องแก้ด้วยวิธีต่างกัน.

T6“final-flow node หยุดเฉพาะเส้นทางของมัน ขณะที่เส้นทางขนานอื่นยังทำงานต่อได้”

“A final-flow node stops only its own path, while other parallel paths can continue.”

2 marks · Lesson 5 · clause 14

จริง. final-flow node (วงกลมมีกากบาท) หยุดเฉพาะ control flow หรือ object flow เส้นนั้น ส่วน final-activity node (bullseye) หยุดทุกเส้นของ activity. ประโยคนี้จึงถูก — และการแยกสองสัญลักษณ์นี้คือจุดที่คนตอบผิดบ่อย.

T7“validation ถามว่าเราสร้างระบบได้ถูกต้องหรือไม่ (did we build it right)”

“Validation asks whether we built the system right.”

2 marks · Lesson 1 · clause 13

เท็จ. “สร้างได้ถูกต้อง” คือ verification; validation ถามว่าเราสร้างระบบที่ถูกหรือไม่ (right system) ตามที่ผู้มีส่วนได้ส่วนเสียต้องการ. ที่ระดับ requirement: validation = นิยามระบบที่ถูกต้องตามเจตนาหรือไม่, verification = requirement เขียนได้ดีหรือไม่ — กลับกันเมื่อไรเสียคะแนนทุกครั้ง.

T8“การกำหนด priority ให้ requirement หมายความว่า requirement นั้นเป็นของที่ไม่จำเป็น”

“Assigning a priority to a requirement means it is optional.”

2 marks · Lesson 2 · clause 14

เท็จ. สไลด์ปฏิเสธตรง ๆ ว่า priority ไม่ได้หมายความว่าบาง requirement ไม่จำเป็น — priority ใช้ระบุพื้นที่ต่อรอง (trade space) และลำดับการทำ. requirement ที่ priority ต่ำอาจเป็นเงื่อนไขที่ requirement อื่นต้องพึ่งก็ได้.

T9“constraint คือคุณลักษณะที่วัดได้ซึ่งบอกว่า requirement นั้นใช้เมื่อใด”

“A constraint is a measurable attribute stating when a requirement applies.”

2 marks · Lesson 2 · clause 10

เท็จ. สิ่งที่บอกว่า requirement ใช้เมื่อใดคือ condition ส่วน constraint คือข้อจำกัดที่ถูกกำหนดจากภายนอก (กฎหมาย งบ แพลตฟอร์ม อินเทอร์เฟซเดิม) และต่อรองไม่ได้. ประโยคนี้สลับคำนิยามทั้งสองคำ.

T10“ใน activity diagram control flow เป็นเส้นทึบ ส่วน object flow เป็นเส้นประ”

“In an activity diagram, a control flow is a solid line and an object flow is dashed.”

2 marks · Lesson 5 · clause 14

จริง. control flow (เส้นทึบ) แสดงลำดับการทำงาน; object flow (เส้นประ) แสดงการไหลของ object ระหว่าง activity. การวาดสลับชนิดเส้นเป็นจุดที่ตรวจง่ายและหักคะแนนทันที.

T11“prototyping กับ throwaway prototyping ต่างกันตรงที่ throwaway คือตัวต้นแบบที่จะพัฒนาต่อไปเป็นระบบจริง”

“Prototyping and throwaway prototyping differ in that the throwaway prototype is the one that evolves into the delivered system.”

2 marks · Lesson 1 · clause 2

เท็จ. กลับกัน: prototyping คือต้นแบบที่พัฒนาต่อไปเป็นระบบจริง ส่วน throwaway ถูกทิ้งหลังใช้ตัดสินใจเรื่อง design. ประโยคนี้สลับชื่อทั้งสองแบบ — มักออกคู่กับคำถามว่าควรใช้แบบไหนเมื่อความต้องการยังไม่ชัด.

T12“observation เป็นวิธีที่แนะนำสำหรับปัญหาความต้องการที่ผู้ใช้พูดออกมาไม่ได้ (unarticulated needs)”

“Observation is the recommended remedy for unarticulated needs.”

2 marks · Lesson 3 · clause 12 and clause 6

จริง. เพราะผู้ใช้ทำงานบางอย่างโดยไม่รู้ตัวและไม่คิดว่าจะต้องบอก — การเฝ้าดูงานจริงเผย workaround ที่การสัมภาษณ์พลาด. เหตุผลที่ต้องใส่คือข้อจำกัด: พฤติกรรมเปลี่ยนเมื่อถูกสังเกต และต้องดูงานที่เป็นรอบ (รายสัปดาห์/รายเดือน) ด้วย.

ข้อ 1ระบบต้องจองคิวง่ายและรวดเร็ว และให้ใช้ Google Calendar ในการจัดการตารางแพทย์ — จงระบุลักษณะที่ผิดสองข้อ แล้วเขียนความต้องการใหม่เป็นสามข้อที่วัดได้

The system must book appointments easily and quickly, and shall use Google Calendar to manage doctors' schedules. Name two characteristics it fails, then rewrite it as three measurable requirements.

6 marks · Lesson 2 · clause 8 and clause 11

Marker wants: two characteristics named; the tool named as premature design; three requirements, each with a number or a named standard.

ผิดสองลักษณะ: ไม่ชัดเจน — “ง่าย” และ “รวดเร็ว” วัดไม่ได้ ไม่มีตัวเลขหรือมาตรฐานอ้างอิง; และ หลายเรื่องในประโยคเดียว — การจองกับการจัดการตารางแพทย์เป็นคนละความต้องการ ส่วน “ใช้ Google Calendar” เป็น premature design เพราะเลือกเครื่องมือก่อนตกลงเรื่องความต้องการ. เขียนใหม่: (1) ระบบยืนยันการจองภายใน 3 วินาทีที่เปอร์เซ็นไทล์ 95 ในเวลาทำการ; (2) ระบบแสดงช่องเวลาว่างของแพทย์ที่เลือกสำหรับ 14 วันข้างหน้า; (3) ระบบบันทึกการนัดพร้อมรหัสผู้ป่วย รหัสแพทย์ เวลาเริ่ม และสถานะ. การเลือกเครื่องมือจัดตารางเป็นเรื่อง design ไม่ใช่ requirement.

ข้อ 2จากข้อเท็จจริงต่อไปนี้ จงจำแนกแต่ละข้อว่าเป็นข้อมูลระบบเดิม ความต้องการผู้มีส่วนได้ส่วนเสีย มาตรฐานองค์กร หรือกฎหมาย แล้วอธิบายว่า PDPA เปลี่ยนความต้องการอย่างไร: (ก) โรงพยาบาลมีระบบนัดหมายเดิมบน Oracle และมีผู้ป่วย 40,000 คน (ข) เจ้าหน้าที่ต้องการเห็นตารางลาของแพทย์ก่อนจอง (ค) โรงพยาบาลกำหนดให้เก็บข้อมูลผู้ป่วยไว้ในประเทศ (ง) PDPA กำหนดให้ขอความยินยอมก่อนส่ง SMS เตือนนัด (จ) คู่มือโรงพยาบาลกำหนดให้บันทึกการนัดทุกครั้งในแฟ้มผู้ป่วย

Tag each fact as existing-system information, stakeholder need, organisational standard or regulation, then explain what the PDPA constraint changes in the requirements.

6 marks · Lesson 1 · clause 7

Marker wants: five correct tags; then the consequence — a new functional requirement plus a constraint plus an alternate flow.

(ก) ข้อมูลระบบเดิม; (ข) ความต้องการผู้มีส่วนได้ส่วนเสีย; (ค) มาตรฐานองค์กร; (ง) กฎหมาย; (จ) มาตรฐานองค์กร. PDPA ไม่ได้เปลี่ยนเพียงถ้อยคำ แต่เพิ่มของใหม่สามอย่าง: requirement ให้บันทึกความยินยอมและทางเลือกไม่รับข้อความ, constraint เรื่องวัตถุประสงค์และระยะเวลาเก็บข้อมูล, และ alternate flow ใน use case เตือนนัด — “ไม่มีความยินยอม → ไม่ส่ง SMS แต่ยังเห็นนัดในแอป”. ถ้าตอบว่า “ต้องขอ consent” เฉย ๆ จะได้ไม่ครบ เพราะไม่ได้บอกว่าความต้องการเปลี่ยนตรงไหน.

ข้อ 3จงจำแนกความต้องการสี่ข้อนี้บนแกนทั้งสี่ของ Lesson 2 (user/system/software · product/process · functional/non-functional · interface) โดยบอกทุกครั้งว่ากำลังใช้แกนใด: (1) แอปต้องทำงานบน Android 10 ขึ้นไป (2) พยาบาลเท่านั้นที่แก้ไขตารางเวรได้ (3) ระบบต้องแลกเปลี่ยนข้อมูลนัดหมายกับ HIS ผ่าน HL7 FHIR (4) ระบบต้องรองรับผู้ใช้พร้อมกัน 500 คน

Q1Describe the four SDLC phases and explain where requirements engineering fits.

Marker wants: four phases in order, the guiding question of each, one deliverable each, RE centred in Analysis but fed by Planning and re-entered in Design.

Planning asks why the system should be built and how the project will be managed; it produces the system request, feasibility study and project plan. Analysis asks who will use it and what it must do; it produces the as-is and to-be models, the requirements specification and the system proposal. Design asks how it will be built; it produces architecture, database and program design specifications. Implementation constructs, tests, installs and supports the system. Requirements engineering is centred on Analysis, starts in Planning (business need, high-level requirements) and continues into Design, because requirements and design are interleaved in practice. Phases may run sequentially, iteratively, incrementally or in parallel.

Q2Why do we need requirements engineering? Support the answer with evidence from the lecture.

Marker wants: goal of RE; cost-of-defect multipliers; CHAOS 2020; failure factors led by inaccurate requirements; 15% figure; a conclusion linking early RE to less rework.

RE exists to produce quality software on time and on budget that meets real needs, by defining the process, activities and techniques that gather requirements and produce an SRS. The economic argument is the relative cost of repairing a defect: 1–2 units at requirements time, 5 at design, 10 at coding, 20 at unit test, 50 at acceptance test and 200 in maintenance — so requirements errors are the most expensive to find late. The Standish CHAOS 2020 report found 31% of projects successful, 50% challenged and 19% failed, and the top failure factor is inaccurate requirements. RE consumes about 15% of development cost but protects the other 85%, and supplies the baseline for estimation, testing and evolution.

Q3Compare waterfall and phased development. Which suits a hospital whose requirements are still unclear?

Marker wants: waterfall sequence, long deliverables, approval gates, difficulty going back; phased versions with most important requirements first; matrix verdict; a justified choice with its risk.

Waterfall makes analysts and users proceed in strict sequence; each phase produces long deliverables, ends when the sponsor approves, and going backwards is possible but extremely difficult. It suits stable, well-understood requirements and minimises requirement changes, but with unclear requirements it locks in mistakes early. Phased development establishes the overall concept, then splits requirements into sequential versions, placing the most important and fundamental requirements in version 1 and analysing further for version 2. A useful system reaches users quickly and users can identify more requirements from using it. For unclear requirements I would choose phased development: the matrix rates waterfall Poor and phased Good on that criterion. The main risks are choosing the wrong features for version 1 and managing expectations, because each version is deliberately incomplete.

Q4A hospital wants an appointment app in three months, with vague requirements and a need for visible progress. Select a methodology and justify it.

Marker wants: the three characteristics named; matrix values compared across at least four methodologies; a defensible choice; one acknowledged trade-off.

The decisive characteristics are unclear user requirements, a short schedule and a need for schedule visibility. On the lecture matrix, waterfall and parallel development are Poor on all three. Phased development is Good, Excellent and Excellent; XP and Scrum are Excellent on all three; prototyping is Excellent for unclear requirements and short schedules but only Good for visibility; throwaway prototyping is Excellent for unclear requirements but only Good for short schedules. I would choose Scrum, provided the hospital can commit a Product Owner and clinical users to sprint reviews: it delivers a working increment every two to four weeks, giving visible progress, and the backlog absorbs new requirements between sprints. Phased development is the fallback if stakeholders cannot attend the ceremonies.

Q5Identify the inputs and outputs of the RE process, using a patient appointment system.

Marker wants: five classic inputs plus constraints and assumptions; a hospital instance of each; three outputs.

Existing systems: paper appointment ledgers, a shared calendar not linked to medical history, the EHR and the SMS gateway. Stakeholder needs: patients want 24/7 booking and reminders; receptionists want a doctor-schedule dashboard; doctors want daily lists with visit reasons; administrators want no-show statistics. Organisational standards: microservices, Agile, weekly code review, ISO/IEC 27001, WCAG 2.1. Regulation: PDPA/GDPR consent management, HIPAA protection of appointment details as PHI. Domain information: new/follow-up/procedure durations, rotating shifts, emergency blackouts, referral rules. Two further inputs are system constraints (existing servers, HL7 FHIR, 2 seconds at 500 users) and assumptions (four hours of training, 99.9% LAN uptime, a ready EHR API). Outputs: agreed requirements, a stakeholder/system/software specification, and system models.

Q6Distinguish system RE from software RE. Why does it matter for a medical device containing hardware?

Marker wants: both definitions; the origin difference; partitioning/allocation; the all-software exception; a device example.

System requirements engineering analyses and documents system requirements; it transforms an operational need into system discipline, performance parameters and system configuration through analysis, design, trade-off studies and prototyping. Software requirements engineering analyses and documents software requirements starting from the system requirements: it partitions system requirements into subsystems and tasks, allocates them to software, and transforms them into software requirements. The major difference is origin: system requirements originate in user needs, whereas software requirements originate in the system requirements and specifications. For a medical device this matters because the system engineer decides what is done in hardware (a pulse-oximeter sensor, a physical alarm) and what in software. That allocation must happen before software requirements can be written, or the SRS will specify behaviour the hardware cannot support. When the system is all or almost all software, the software engineer effectively becomes the system engineer.

Q7Describe the five RE activities and explain why the activity model is not a simple sequence.

Marker wants: elicitation, analysis and negotiation, specification, validation, management; feedback arrows; management spanning all; an example of validation sending work back.

Elicitation discovers requirements from stakeholders, documents, domain knowledge and market studies. Analysis and negotiation examine them in detail, classify and model them, allocate them to architecture, and resolve conflicts. Specification documents the agreed requirements at an appropriate level of detail in natural language and diagrams. Validation checks consistency and completeness through modelling, reviews, prototyping, acceptance testing and, for critical systems, formal verification. Management ensures requirements are identified, documented, maintained, communicated and traced throughout the life cycle. The model is not a simple sequence because validation has feedback arrows back to analysis and elicitation, and analysis feeds back to elicitation. When a review finds that “queue number” means different things to patients and staff, the process returns to negotiation. Management is drawn as a bracket spanning activities one to four, because change control and traceability apply to all of them.

Q8Distinguish verification from validation, and requirements verification from requirements validation.

Marker wants: IEEE 610.12 definitions; right-system vs right-build gloss; ISO 29148 requirements-level definitions; an applied example of each.

Verification evaluates a system or component to determine whether the system of a given development phase satisfies the conditions imposed at the start of that phase — were we built to specification? Validation evaluates a system during or at the end of development to determine whether it satisfies specified requirements — did we build what the user needed? At requirements level, ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148 defines requirements verification as confirmation that the requirements, individually and as a set, are well formed, and requirements validation as confirmation that they define the right system as intended by stakeholders. For example, checking that “the dashboard shall render within 2 seconds at 500 concurrent users” is measurable and does not contradict the 50 TPS booking requirement is verification. Asking the pharmacy manager whether two seconds actually solves the queue problem is validation.

Lesson 2

Twelve questions. These are the ones the written paper leans on.

Q1Define a well-formed requirement and explain its four elements.

Marker wants: the ISO-style definition, all four clauses, one example showing each.

A well-formed requirement is a statement that (1) can be verified, (2) must be met or possessed by a system to solve a stakeholder problem or achieve an objective, (3) is qualified by measurable conditions and bounded by constraints, and (4) defines the performance or capability of the system — not a capability of the user, operator or other stakeholder. For example: “When a patient checks in by QR code, the appointment system shall set the appointment status to Arrived within 2 seconds.” It is verifiable by timing the status change; it is a system obligation, not a patient duty; the condition is the QR check-in and the constraint is two seconds; and it describes system behaviour. “The nurse shall check drug interactions” fails element four.

Q2Rewrite: “The system shall be fast and user-friendly.”

Marker wants: split into at least two singular, measurable requirements naming a metric or standard.

The original violates singular (two requirements joined by “and”) and unambiguous (both adjectives are subjective). Split and quantify: (1) “The system shall load and render the dashboard interface in less than 2.0 seconds under normal network conditions.” (2) “The interface shall conform to WCAG 2.1 Level AA and achieve a minimum System Usability Scale score of 80 during user testing.” Each now has a test method — stopwatch or APM for load time, accessibility audit and SUS questionnaire for usability — so each is verifiable. Do not replace “fast” with another vague word such as “responsive”: the correction must introduce a number or a named standard.

Q3Distinguish user, system and software requirements with a hospital example.

Marker wants: definitions, the hierarchy, the regulator/no-human-source point, an example chain.

User requirements are the requirements of the system's customers or end-users. System requirements cover the system as a whole: user requirements plus those of other stakeholders such as regulatory authorities plus requirements with no identifiable human source. Software requirements are derived from system requirements where the system contains software. Example: a doctor wants a faster prescribing screen (user requirement). The pharmacy committee and PDPA require an audit of every prescription (system requirement with no single human owner). The software requirement is “The prescribing module shall write an immutable audit record containing user ID, patient ID, drug, dose and timestamp.” The hierarchy matters because system requirements include people, hardware and legal obligations that user interviews never surface.

Q4Classify each and name the subtype where relevant: (a) patient cancels an appointment; (b) runs on the existing Oracle database; (c) 99.9% monthly availability; (d) sends the booking to the FHIR server within 5 s.

Marker wants: F/NFR plus technology constraint / QoS / interface, with justification; the point that axes are independent.

(a) Functional — it describes a capability the system performs. (b) Non-functional, technology constraint — it mandates a named database engine and constrains implementation rather than describing behaviour. (c) Non-functional, quality-of-service constraint — availability expressed as a measurable level. (d) Functional (it performs a transfer), also an external interface requirement, and the five-second bound is a quality constraint. The exam point is that the axes are independent: one requirement can be functional and an interface requirement at once, so always state which axis you are using. NFRs constrain the solution and usually drive the architecture.

Q5Name four NFR subtypes and give a measurable example of each.

Marker wants: at least performance, security, usability, reliability; each with a number or standard.

Performance: “The system shall complete high-priority search queries within 1.5 seconds at 500 concurrent active users.” Reliability: “The system shall maintain 99.9% operational availability per calendar month, excluding scheduled maintenance.” Security: “The system shall terminate a session and require re-authentication after 15 minutes of inactivity” and “shall encrypt data at rest with AES-256 and in transit with TLS 1.3.” Usability: “The interface shall conform to WCAG 2.1 Level AA and achieve a SUS score of at least 80.” Maintainability could be added as “a defect shall be correctable within 8 person-hours median.” Each must be measurable, otherwise it is a wish, not a requirement. The other NFR subtype is the technology constraint, such as a mandated database.

Q6Why does “The system shall accept an ATM card and PIN for user identification” violate a characteristic? Rewrite it.

Marker wants: singularity, the conjunction, a correct split into a workflow, and the warning not to over-split.

It violates singular: one statement contains multiple behaviours joined by “and”, so it cannot be separately prioritised, estimated, traced or verified — part can pass while another fails. It also hides a sequence. Rewrite as: (1) “The system shall allow a user to insert an ATM card into the card slot.” (2) “After a card is inserted, the system shall verify the card.” (3) “If the card is valid, the system shall display a message prompting the user to enter the PIN.” Split at separable outcomes, not mechanically at every conjunction: “email and password” is one authentication method and stays together. Multi-step behaviour may also be documented in a use case description rather than the requirement list.

Q7Explain “implementation free” using the search-engine example, and state the legitimate exception.

Marker wants: what-not-how; no algorithm/UI/query format; design freedom; externally imposed constraints as the exception.

Implementation free means the requirement states what is required, not how it should be met, avoiding unnecessary constraints on architectural design. The slide's example is “The system shall provide a search result that match user needs.” A violating version would specify the query format, name a matching algorithm such as TF-IDF or a particular embedding model, or dictate how results are displayed. Those are design decisions that should follow trade-offs, and freezing them early can produce a worse or more expensive solution. It matters because it preserves the solution space and lets designers respond to NFRs. The exception is a genuine externally imposed constraint — for example the hospital already owns an Oracle licence — which is legitimately recorded as a technology constraint, not as an implementation-free functional requirement.

Q8Distinguish “necessary” from “feasible”, using the 100%-accuracy example.

Marker wants: necessity = essential/not obsolete; feasibility = achievable within constraints; 100% is a feasibility failure; a realistic correction.

Necessary means the requirement defines an essential capability, characteristic, constraint or quality factor: if removed, a deficiency exists that no other capability can fill, and it is not obsolete. Feasible means it is technically achievable without major technology advances and fits cost, schedule, technical, legal and regulatory constraints with acceptable risk. The slide example — “The system shall detect weapons and animals with 100% accuracy” — violates feasible, not necessary: detection is essential, but no technology achieves 100%, and the requirement implies unlimited cost. The correction is a realistic target such as 98% accuracy, or better, a named metric on an agreed dataset: recall ≥ 98% and false-positive rate ≤ 2%.

Q9Explain the four characteristics of a set of requirements and define requirements creep.

Marker wants: complete (no TBD/TBS/TBR), consistent, affordable, bounded; the creep link; the point that these are set properties.

A set is complete when it contains everything pertinent to the system definition and has no To Be Defined, To Be Specified or To Be Resolved clauses; TBx items may be resolved iteratively within a risk-based timeframe. It is consistent when no requirements contradict each other, none are duplicated, and the same term is used for the same item throughout. It is affordable when the complete set can be satisfied by a solution obtainable within life-cycle constraints — cost, schedule, technical, legal and regulatory. It is bounded when it stays within the identified scope and does not grow beyond user needs. Failing these produces requirements creep: uncontrolled growth during the life cycle that impacts cost, schedule or quality. These are properties of the whole set; an individual line cannot be “affordable” or “bounded”.

Q10Correct the modals and voice: “The appointment system should be built as a mobile app and must not allow double booking.”

Marker wants: should→shall, must→shall, negative→positive, split, active voice, measurable.

“Should” marks a preference or goal and is non-binding, so it cannot be accepted or tested. “Must” is not used because it can be misread as a requirement, and “must not” is a negative statement, which the guidance says to avoid. Rewrite as two singular, binding, positive, active requirements: (1) “The system shall be implemented as a native mobile application for Android and iOS.” (2) “The system shall permit at most one active appointment per doctor per time slot and shall reject a conflicting booking with an error message within 2 seconds.” The second replaces the negative with a positive behaviour and adds a measurable response time, making it verifiable.

Q11Distinguish conditions from constraints, with one hospital example of each.

Marker wants: condition qualifies/measures a requirement; constraint is externally imposed; scope of constraints; examples.

A condition is a measurable qualitative or quantitative attribute stipulated for a requirement; it qualifies the requirement so it can be validated and verified, for example “under a concurrent load of 500 active users” or “during regular service hours”. A constraint is an externally imposed limitation on the system, its design or implementation, or on the development process — for example “The system shall store all patient data within Thailand in accordance with PDPA” or “The project budget shall not exceed 5 million baht”. Constraints may apply to all requirements (UI theme, response time), one requirement, a set of requirements, or stand alone (cost, schedule). The distinction matters because a condition is wording you add for testability, while a constraint is a boundary you cannot design away.

Q12Differentiate iterative from recursive application of processes, using the car-parking system.

Marker wants: same level vs successive lower level; allocation and derived requirements; parallel with architecture; the parking example.

Iterative application repeats the same process or set of processes at the same level. At the whole-system level of the car-parking system, the analyst repeatedly applies requirements identification, analysis and architectural design to identify the elements — entering gate and exiting gate — and their requirements. Recursive application applies the same set of processes to successive lower levels of system elements. The analyst divides the parking system into entering and exiting gate systems, then applies the same process inside the entering gate to define its components (control appliance, long-range reader, CCD camera, gate barrier), their requirements, and the interface requirements with the exiting gate. Iteration resolves requirement/architecture trade-offs at one level; recursion produces derived requirements and lower-level specifications. Recursion can also be bi-directional.

Q1Define requirements elicitation and explain where it sits in the RE lifecycle.

Marker wants: a definition (not just “gathering”); the aim; the four quadrants; the loop with analysis; the draft hand-off.

Requirements elicitation is the activity whose aim is discovering “the requirements” of a system. Developers work with customers and end users, study documents and domain knowledge, and perform market studies to find the problem to be solved, the services stakeholders need, performance requirements and operational constraints. It is a knowledge-transfer process: the engineer elicits and documents existing customer knowledge and need. In the lifecycle it is one quadrant, alongside analysis, specification and validation, with management spanning all four. Elicitation feeds the requirements specification (functional and non-functional) and the analysis model, and loops back to analysis as understanding improves. Its output is a draft requirements document, which is analysed for problems, conflicts and overlaps and negotiated before it becomes an agreed specification.

Q2What is problem analysis, and why should it precede elicitation?

Marker wants: the slide 8 definition; the three activities; the goal; a consequence of skipping it; an example.

Problem analysis is “the activity that encompasses learning about the problem to be solved (often through brainstorming and/or questioning), understanding the needs of potential users, trying to find out who the user really is, and understanding all the constraints of the solution”. Its goal is to understand the problem before development begins; the deck calls elicitation a process of problem analysis and understanding. It must come first, otherwise a team may run excellent interviews and document analysis against the wrong problem. For example, a clinic asking for an “AI triage app” may actually have a registration-form defect: the owner's name and area code are missing, causing duplicate records and failed reminders. Problem analysis reveals that the real requirements concern form design and data validation, not AI.

Q3Identify stakeholder classes for a hospital appointment system and explain why a cross-section is needed.

Marker wants: at least five classes; internal vs external; roles, expectations, constraints; the bias argument; a conflict example.

Classes include patients (external end users who book and view appointments); doctors (internal end users who provide care and record availability, 8 AM–5 PM); receptionists (who book for patients and know workarounds); nurses or clinic staff (who prepare the schedule); hospital management (access and utilisation targets); IT operations and the registration-system owner (interfaces); and regulatory bodies (lawful handling of patient data). Each has different expectations and constraints: a doctor wants long enough slots and control of availability; management wants utilisation; patients want convenience; reception wants a simple override. Slide 23 warns that information from one class or one level is biased from a single perspective, so a representative cross-section is necessary for the true picture. Without receptionists, the team would miss the paper workaround for patients without an e-patient card.

Q4Compare interviews and observation for eliciting requirements for a hospital appointment system. Two strengths and two weaknesses each.

Marker wants: fair comparison on depth, breadth, as-is/to-be, bias, cost; two each; a recommendation to combine.

Interviews are scheduled conversations with selected stakeholders using open, closed and probing questions, followed by a report the interviewee verifies. Strengths: high depth, so the engineer can probe why receptionists override slots and explore both as-is and to-be; and they allow clarification and build trust. Weaknesses: low breadth, so one interview is one perspective; and they depend on what people remember and are willing to say, so workarounds may be hidden. Observation watches the work environment and patterns. Strengths: it reveals tacit work users forget to mention, such as phoning the ward to confirm a discharge bed, and it validates interview data; training needs are low. Weaknesses: behaviour changes when people are watched, and periodic weekly/monthly/annual activities are easily missed if observation is too short. Observe first, then interview about what was seen.

Q5Describe the five steps of the interview process and explain why the post-interview report matters.

Marker wants: all five steps with content; the 48-hour rule; verification by the interviewee; separating facts from opinions; the validation link.

The steps are: (1) select interviewees and create a schedule based on information needs, spanning senior managers, mid-level managers, staff and users; (2) design open-ended, closed-ended and probing questions; (3) prepare by confirming the knowledge area, choosing structured or unstructured format, anticipating answers, preparing follow-ups and briefing the interviewee; (4) conduct using top-down and bottom-up questioning, record everything, clarify, summarise periodically, separate opinions from facts and promise nothing prematurely; (5) follow up with an interview report within 48 hours, sent to the interviewee to verify. The report matters because memory fades and notes are ambiguous; the interviewee can correct misunderstandings, which is early requirements validation. It also records open items and next steps, turning a conversation into a traceable source.

Q6You must elicit requirements from 500 patients across three hospitals. Choose a technique, state its limitations, and mitigate them.

Marker wants: questionnaire; justification by breadth/cost; response rates; design rules; mitigation; combination.

A questionnaire is the appropriate primary technique: a set of written questions used to obtain information from individuals, paper or electronic, designed for large numbers and for information plus opinions. The matrix gives it high breadth at low cost with low user involvement. It cannot give depth: response rates are typically below 50% for paper and below 30% for web, ambiguous items cannot be probed, and non-response may bias the sample. To mitigate, use a representative sample, offer paper and online versions, explain why the respondent was selected, state a deadline, offer a summary of results, hand out forms personally and contact non-respondents. Pretest the questionnaire, avoid biased items and provide anonymity. Complement it with interviews of receptionists and clinicians and document analysis of the appointment form, because it cannot reveal workflow or exceptions.

Q7Compare JAD with interviews. When is JAD worth its higher coordination cost?

Marker wants: definitions; roles; process; matrix values; when to choose which.

Interviews are one-to-one, high-depth, low-breadth and low-integration: good for understanding one role's work, weak at reconciling views. JAD is “structured workshops with brainstorming” in which managers, users and developers work together in a prepared room, led by a neutral facilitator who does not participate, with a scribe recording decisions. It typically runs 5–10 days over three weeks and produces a post-session report within 1–2 weeks. The matrix gives JAD high depth, high integration and high user involvement at low–medium cost, with medium breadth. JAD is worth the cost when departments have conflicting rules — cardiology, paediatrics and radiology disagreeing about slots — because it resolves conflict in the room and produces an agreed scope. It is not worth it for a single role's detailed workflow (interview) or for surveying hundreds of patients (questionnaire).

Q8Using the veterinary clinic card, explain document analysis and the requirements it reveals.

Marker wants: definition; document types; the three findings; the as-is limitation; resulting to-be requirements.

Document analysis studies existing documents — forms, reports, memos, policy manuals, training manuals, organisation charts, regulations and user interfaces — for clues about the as-is system. It looks for users' own forms and additions, and for unused forms and fields. The veterinary clinic card shows three findings. First, the customer wrote the pet's name “Buffy” in the Name field, so the field should be labelled “Owner's Name”. Second, staff added the animal type and date of birth (“Collie 7/6/99”) on the Pet's Name line, so the new form needs both. Third, the phone number lacked an area code, so the form needs an explicit area code. These become form-design and validation requirements. Document analysis is cheap and low-training, but it describes only the as-is, may be outdated, and cannot capture tacit workarounds, so combine it with observation and interviews.

Q9What is a scenario? List its five elements and illustrate them with booking an appointment.

Marker wants: definition; all five elements; a concrete example of each; the purpose.

A scenario is “an example of an interaction session concerned with a single type of interaction between an end-user and the system”; it is a story of how the system is used, mainly for adding detail to an outline requirements description. It should contain the state before the scenario, the normal flow, exceptions, other activities happening at the same time, and the state after completion. For appointment booking: before, the patient has a discharged visit, an open referral and is logged in. Normal flow: select department, view available slots, choose a slot, confirm, receive confirmation. Exception: the slot is taken between viewing and confirming, so the system offers alternatives. Concurrent activity: the doctor changes availability while the patient is booking. After: the appointment is recorded, a reminder is scheduled and the registration system is updated.

Q10Write one functional and one non-functional user story for an appointment system, and explain acceptance criteria and the backlog.

Marker wants: correct form; one functional, one non-functional; customer-written acceptance criteria; backlog prioritisation; story/task relation.

Functional: “As a patient, I want to book an appointment with my preferred doctor so that I can receive care at a convenient time.” Non-functional: “As a receptionist, I want the daily appointment schedule to print on the shared clinic printer so that I do not need a cable connection to each computer.” Both follow the form: a short description from the perspective of the person who wants the feature. Before implementation the customer writes an acceptance procedure; for the booking story, given an available slot, when the patient confirms, then the appointment is saved and a confirmation is sent. Stories go into a product backlog prioritised by importance, risk and effort, and each gets a task list; if the list is long, the story is split. They are low-tech, portable and easily updated.

Q11Name three problems faced by requirements engineers in elicitation and give a solution for each.

Marker wants: three distinct problems from slide 31; accurate definitions; specific solutions; an example.

First, the communication gap: developers and users use different vocabularies, so requirements are misinterpreted or missed. Solutions are a shared glossary, avoiding technical jargon with business users, and prototyping with wireframes instead of heavy text. Second, unarticulated needs: users cannot describe what they need and assume the system should “just know”. Solutions are job shadowing, the 5 Whys to reach the root problem, and reverse-engineering existing forms, reports and legacy workflows. Third, organisational politics: departments have conflicting objectives and KPIs, and stakeholders push features for influence. Solutions are stakeholder analysis to map influence and impact, a neutral facilitator, and a steering committee that decides on corporate strategy. In a hospital, shadowing a ward clerk may reveal that bed status is phoned to admissions; 5 Whys shows the real need is a single source of truth updated at discharge.

Q12Describe the generic elicitation process and explain what happens to its output.

Marker wants: four stages and sub-activities; knowledge organisation; draft document; analysis and negotiation; link to specification.

The process has four stages. First, establish objectives: business goals, the problem to be solved, and system constraints such as budget, schedule and interoperability. Second, understand the background: organisational structure and roles, the application domain, and the existing system through document analysis, observation and manuals. Third, organise knowledge: identify stakeholders, prioritise goals, filter out domain knowledge that does not contribute to requirements, and collate against a checklist, possibly using an ontology. Fourth, collect stakeholder requirements by consulting stakeholders and deriving domain and organisational requirements. The output is a draft document describing the system requirements. It is then analysed to discover problems, conflicts and overlaps, and a negotiation stage is required to reach agreed requirements. Elicitation and analysis loop, and the result feeds the specification and the analysis model.

Q13How would you select and combine elicitation techniques for a hospital appointment system?

Marker wants: seven criteria; matrix values; a justified mix; coverage of as-is, to-be, depth, breadth, integration.

Selection uses seven criteria: type, depth, breadth and integration of information, user involvement, cost, and combining techniques. For a hospital appointment system I would start with document analysis of the paper form, registration policy and reports: low cost, little training, and it reveals as-is fields and business rules. I would observe a clinic morning for workarounds and periodic activities, then interview the reception manager, two doctors and a patient representative for depth and to-be ideas. I would run a JAD with department representatives and IT to resolve conflicting slot rules, because JAD gives high integration and user involvement. I would survey patients across clinics for breadth, accepting lower depth and response-rate limits. I would write scenarios for normal and exception flows and user stories for the backlog. No single technique covers all criteria, so the mix is deliberate.

Q14What is scope creep, and how can elicitation and scope management prevent it?

Marker wants: definition; causes; three solutions; the scope-as-critical-element link; an example.

Scope creep is uncontrolled feature growth because system boundaries are vague and users keep requesting additions, leading to budget and schedule overruns. Slide 38 gives three solutions: a signed-off scope baseline at the start; context diagrams that explicitly show what is in and out of scope; and a formal change-control process that assesses impact before approving changes. Slide 10 adds that elicitation should inform project scope by describing the software and its purpose and prioritising deliverables so the most important business needs come first; the description should be extensible and compatible with later requirements, and requirements should be classified by type attributes. For example, an appointment system's scope might include booking, cancellation and reminders and explicitly exclude billing and in-patient scheduling; a request to add billing then goes through change control rather than being absorbed silently.

Lesson 4

Sixteen questions. Analysis, negotiation and the models that support them.

Q1Define requirements analysis and negotiation, and name the five faults it is designed to find.

Marker wants: the objective (agreed requirements conforming to the characteristics); the five faults; the distinction from elicitation.

Requirements analysis and negotiation is the process that establishes requirements agreed by the stakeholders and conforming to the requirements characteristics — complete, unambiguous and consistent. It examines the draft produced by elicitation and deliberately looks for five faults: missing requirements, requirements that conflict with each other, ambiguous requirements, overlapping requirements, and unrealistic requirements. Matching faults are then taken to negotiation, where stakeholders discuss the conflict, prioritise, and agree a modification or simplification. It is not a second elicitation: no new needs are being collected; existing statements are checked, elaborated and agreed. Cleland-Huang groups the work into conceptual modelling, allocation to architecture, and conflict identification.

Q2State the five objectives of requirements analysis and apply each to a hospital appointment system.

Marker wants: five named objectives, each with a concrete application; no generic restatement.

1) Discover and clarify. “Remind the patient” becomes: push notification 24 hours before, then SMS after five minutes without confirmation.
2) Evaluate feasibility. A request to predict no-shows with 100% accuracy is rejected; the measurable target becomes 80% recall on last year's data.
3) Resolve conflicts. Reception wants 10-minute slots, doctors want 15; negotiation settles 12 minutes plus one overbooked slot per hour.
4) Define project scope. Booking, cancellation, reminders and doctor availability are in; billing and in-patient scheduling are explicitly out.
5) Map system architecture. Reminders are allocated to the notification component, booking to the scheduling component, patient data to the records component.

Q3What four results does an analysis phase produce, and why does each matter?

Marker wants: four named results and a distinct use for each.

SRS — the formal, systematic document that is the official agreement between client and team; it is what scope and acceptance are measured against.
Analysis models — use case, class, activity, data-flow and entity-relationship diagrams; they make assumptions visible and expose missing interactions.
Prioritised requirements list — ranks by urgency and value, so the first release contains what matters and the rest can wait.
Traceability matrix and architecture diagram — show which component satisfies which requirement, proving coverage and supporting impact analysis when something changes.

Q4Describe the analysis and negotiation process, naming the output of each check and of each negotiation step.

Marker wants: three checks in order; four failure names; three negotiation outputs; the loop-back.

Analysis runs three checks. Necessity checking produces unnecessary requirements. Consistency and completeness checking produces incomplete and conflicting requirements. Feasibility checking produces infeasible requirements.
Negotiation then handles them in three steps: requirements discussion, requirements prioritisation, requirements agreement. If agreement is not reached, the requirement returns to the checks; if it is, the agreed version becomes the baseline. The order matters: a requirement cannot be judged feasible before it is known to be necessary and complete.

Q5Explain the three fundamental ways of structuring requirements knowledge, using the lecture's museum examples.

Marker wants: three named structures with the relationship each expresses, one museum example each, and what gap each exposes.

Partitioning (whole–part). Break a complex entity into its structural parts. The artifact profile becomes unique ID, title, artist/creator, creation date, medium, historical description, 3D model file and physical origin. It exposes a missing part.
Abstraction (general–specific). Group specific kinds under one general concept. “Museum exhibit” covers oil paintings, marble sculptures, textiles, coins and digital installations. It exposes a missing kind.
Projection (viewpoints). Organise by how different parties see the system: digital curators (upload and metadata), visitors (3D navigation, audio guides), educators (quizzes, tour paths), IT administrators (privacy, access control, bandwidth), and the collections database as a system view. It exposes a missing viewpoint. Davis presents all three as methods that help analysis, not as alternatives — a complete analysis may use all three.

Q6Name the six classification dimensions and classify one requirement on each.

Marker wants: six dimensions named; a requirement placed on every axis; the emergent/derived distinction.

Take “The system shall send a reminder 24 hours before the appointment”.
1) Functional or non-functional: functional.
2) Origin: derived from the business goal of cutting no-shows — not imposed and not emergent.
3) Product or process: product.
4) Priority: mandatory for the first release.
5) Scope: narrow — it belongs to the notification function.
6) Volatility: stable; the need to remind is part of the domain.
Contrast “The system shall be a responsive PWA conforming to WCAG 2.1 AA”: non-functional, imposed, product, mandatory, global, stable. An emergent property is one the whole produces and no part has, such as an ATM's deposit/withdraw/transfer emerging from its components.

Q7Distinguish global from narrow scope, and classify four requirements of a museum app.

Marker wants: definitions; four classifications; why global requirements are decided early.

A global requirement affects the whole software; its satisfaction cannot be allocated to a discrete component and it shapes the architecture. A narrow requirement is local to one function or screen, leaves design freedom and has little impact elsewhere.
Global: “a responsive progressive web app across desktop, tablet and phone, conforming to WCAG 2.1 AA” — it dictates the framework, layout and every interface.
Narrow: “a search bar on the gallery home page filtering by era”; “save a favourite's ID to the session”; “show a 3D model within 2 seconds on a 10 Mbps connection”.
Global requirements are settled early because every later design choice inherits them; retrofitting accessibility or responsiveness re-opens the architecture. Narrow ones can be deferred or changed cheaply.

Q8List the eight analysis checklist items and apply three of them to this requirement: “The system shall let visitors search by name, comment, report a typo and rate an exhibit out of five stars.”

Marker wants: the eight items; three correctly applied; corrected requirements.

The eight items: conformance with business goals · premature design · combined requirements · unnecessary requirements · use of non-standard hardware/software · ambiguity · realism · testability. A checklist should hold no more than ten items.
Combined: the sentence bundles four independent functions — search, comment, report a typo, rate. Split into four requirements so each can be tested and traced.
Testability: “rate out of five stars” is testable, but the bundle as a whole cannot pass or fail as one item; after splitting, state the range and persistence — “a rating from 1 to 5, stored with the exhibit and the visitor's session”.
Ambiguity: “comment” does not say whether authentication or moderation is required; specify “authenticated visitors may submit text comments on an artifact detail page”.
The correction therefore adds structure, measurable conditions and a clear actor, not new vague words.

Q9Explain the interaction matrix, its three codes, and what you do with each result. Use the slide's R1–R6 matrix.

Marker wants: purpose; the three codes; a correct reading of one row; the action for each code.

An interaction matrix compares every requirement with every other to expose conflicts and overlaps that a flat list hides. Fill each cell with 1 for a conflict, 1000 for an overlap and 0 for independence. Reading row R3 of the slide's matrix: R3 overlaps R1, R4 and R6 (three cells of 1000) and is independent of R2 and R5 (two cells of 0).
Action: every 1 goes to negotiation, because two requirements cannot both be satisfied as written; every 1000 is merged, deduplicated or linked as a shared requirement; every 0 is left alone. The matrix is symmetric — R1·R3 and R3·R1 carry the same code — so only half the table needs reading. The codes are labels, not magnitudes: 1000 does not mean “a worse conflict”.

Q10Why build conceptual models, how is the notation chosen, and which model would you use for capabilities, objects and process flow?

Marker wants: purpose (understanding as-is and to-be); three notation factors; three model-to-question mappings.

Conceptual models describe entities of the problem domain and their relationships. They aid understanding of the situation in which the problem occurs (as-is) and depict the proposed solution (to-be); they are how a misreading becomes visible before it is coded.
Notation is chosen on three factors: the nature of the problem (real-time software favours state and parametric models, typically SysML; information systems favour object and activity models, typically UML); the engineer's expertise; and the customer's process requirements, which may mandate or forbid a notation.
Capabilities and actors → use case diagram. Objects and their relationships → class diagram. Process flow → activity diagram (with sequence and state-machine diagrams for behaviour over time).

Q11Explain requirements allocation with the search-engine example, and say what it discovers.

Marker wants: definition; the four allocations; the interface requirements that appear; the coverage argument.

Allocation is the assignment of each requirement to the architecture component responsible for satisfying it, performed during analysis because analysis cannot proceed far without knowing where a requirement lives. For a search engine whose objective is relevant results: the query module accepts keywords from the user; the ranking module returns results sorted by relevance score; the indexing module updates the index in batch every 24 hours; the crawler controller gives each crawler a predefined list of websites.
Allocation discovers interface requirements nobody stated: the ranking module needs the query module's parsed terms; the indexer needs a batch window; the crawler needs a site list from configuration. It also proves coverage — every requirement has an owner — and supports impact analysis when a component changes.

Q12What is formal analysis, what two benefits does it bring, and when is it used?

Marker wants: formal semantics; precision and proof; the application domain; at least one language family.

Formal analysis expresses requirements in a language with formally defined semantics. It gives two benefits: requirements are stated precisely and unambiguously, removing the potential for misinterpretation; and they can be reasoned over, so desired properties of the software can be proved rather than argued.
It has made an impact in high-integrity systems — safety-critical medical devices, for example — where a proof is worth the cost. Languages split into algebraic and model-based, sequential and concurrent: Larch and OBJ (algebraic, sequential), Z, VDM and B (model-based, sequential), Lotos (algebraic, concurrent), CSP and Petri nets (model-based, concurrent). A requirement written formally is not “more detailed prose”; without formal semantics the proof benefit disappears.

Q13What causes requirements conflicts, what governs the compromise, and how should a negotiation meeting run?

Marker wants: three sources; four governing factors; three stages; the neutral chair and the recorded trade-off.

Conflicts arise between two stakeholders wanting mutually incompatible features, between requirements and resources, and between functional and non-functional requirements. The final requirements are a compromise governed by four things: the needs of the organisation in general, the specific requirements of different stakeholders, design and implementation constraints, and the budget and schedule.
Meetings are the most effective method. They run in three stages: information — explain the nature of the problem; discussion — the stakeholders involved discuss how to resolve it; resolution — agree the actions. Participants receive the analysis results (for example the completed checklists), and the meeting is chaired by someone who is not a stakeholder. The resolution must record what each side gave up, otherwise the conflict returns as a change request.

Q14Explain cost/benefit prioritisation with the bank example, justify the order, and say what AHP adds.

Marker wants: the two scores; the decision; the criterion (penalty dominance); AHP pairwise comparison.

Cost/benefit analysis scores each requirement on the value of implementing it and the penalty of omitting it, on a 1–5 scale. Requirement A, biometric login: value 5 (convenience, competitiveness) and penalty 2 (users complain, but the current login works). Requirement B, compliance with the new data-protection law: value 1 (customers notice nothing) and penalty 5 (fines, app-store suspension, reputational damage).
Decision: B is done first. The extreme penalty makes it mandatory; A is highly desirable but can wait a sprint if resources run out. Note that the slide prints totals of 7 for A and 6 for B yet still orders B first — the sum is not the rule; an extreme penalty dominates a convenience benefit, and the answer should say so.
AHP adds structured comparison: every unique pair of requirements is compared to decide which is higher priority and by how much, producing a consistent ranking when there are many requirements.

Q15Compare BPA, BPI and BPR on benefit, cost, breadth and risk, and recommend one for a hospital's paper-based booking process.

Marker wants: three definitions; the four-row comparison; a justified recommendation tied to risk appetite.

BPA uses technology to automate some existing work: least change, low risk, low payoff, and much time spent understanding the as-is. BPI makes moderate changes to improve the efficiency of the as-is, often copying a competitor. BPR is a complete makeover focused on the to-be, with little time on the as-is: high risk, high payoff.
The comparison: potential benefit low–moderate / moderate / high; project cost low / low–moderate / high; breadth of analysis narrow / narrow–moderate / very broad; risk low–moderate / low–moderate / very high.
Recommendation: start with BPI for paper-based booking — replace the paper diary with a shared electronic schedule and redesign the queue so registration and booking share one flow. It improves efficiency without the very high risk of reengineering. If the hospital's strategy is to remove walk-in booking entirely and lead with telemedicine, that is BPR, and it must be justified by the risk the organisation can carry, not by novelty.

Q16Name the seven analysis strategies, choose one for three scenarios, and name the four common analysis mistakes.

Marker wants: seven named strategies; three justified choices; four mistakes with their consequence.

The seven: problem analysis, root-cause analysis, duration analysis, activity-based costing, informal benchmarking, outcome analysis and technology analysis.
Scenarios. Frequent stock-outs: root-cause analysis — users list prioritised problems and all possible causes; the analyst finds common causes. A clinic visit that takes 90 minutes but only 20 minutes of clinical work: duration analysis — compare step time with total time and integrate or parallelise. An insurance claim that ends with the customer arranging their own repair: outcome analysis — start from the desired outcome (a repaired car) and extend the process to contract an authorised repair shop.
Four mistakes: reducing analysis time (builds the wrong product), requirements gold plating (scope creep, missed deadline and budget), developer gold plating (unrequested technical features), and lack of user involvement (a technically working system that users reject). Each trades a small saving now for a large cost later.

Lesson 5

Fourteen questions. Use cases, descriptions and activity diagrams.

Q1Name the three representations of the functional model and say what question each answers.

Marker wants: three named views; the question each answers; the logical/implementation-independent point.

Use case diagram — what functionality the system offers, to which actors, inside which boundary (scope).
Use case description — for one use case, how it unfolds: trigger, actors, normal and alternate flows (detail).
Activity diagram — the business process across activities and roles: decisions, parallel work, who does what (sequence). All three are logical: they do not commit to manual or computerised implementation.

Q2List the five elements of a use case diagram and explain where the boundary goes.

Marker wants: five elements; actors outside, use cases inside; the system name on the boundary.

Actors and actor relationships; use cases and use case relationships; the interaction (association) between actor and use case; the system boundary; the system name. Actors sit outside the boundary because they are external to the system; use cases sit inside it; the system name labels the boundary. The boundary is a scope decision: the SMS gateway is an actor, sending the message is a use case.

Q3How do you find actors, what kinds exist, and what are the four actor types?

Marker wants: the finding questions; five kinds; four types with one example each.

Ask who or what produces or consumes the system's data: who uses the main functions, who needs support, who maintains it, which devices it handles, which systems it interacts with, who cares about the result. Kinds: human, machine, external system, organisational unit, sensor. Types: primary business (benefits — employee paid), primary system (initiates — teller enters a deposit), external server (responds — credit bureau authorises), external receiver (gets value — warehouse receives a packing slip).

Q4Explain association multiplicity and actor generalisation, and correct the common misreading of the arrowhead.

Marker wants: no arrowhead = two-way, solid = one-way; generalisation = substitution; arrow ≠ initiation.

Multiplicity is the number of associations. No arrowhead means information flows both ways; a solid arrowhead means one way. The arrow shows the direction of information, not who initiates the use case — initiation is recorded by the primary actor in the description. Actor generalisation means instances of the specialised actor may substitute for the general one, inheriting its associations (a nurse can do what a staff member can do).

Q5Give the six-step procedure for creating a use case diagram and explain “right size”.

Marker wants: six ordered steps including the review/split-combine step; one sizing example.

1) Review the requirements definition. 2) Identify the boundary, primary actors and their goals, business processes and major use cases. 3) Carefully review the set — split or combine to the right size, and add missing cases. 4) Place and draw the use cases and actors. 5) Draw the subject boundary. 6) Add the associations. “Right size” means each ellipse is one goal a user can complete in one sitting (sea level): “Manage appointment” is too coarse; “Book”, “Reschedule”, “Cancel” are the right size, with “Remind patient” separate.

Q6Distinguish include, extend and generalisation, giving the arrow direction for each.

Marker wants: required vs optional; arrow from dependent to dependency; one example each; direction.

Include — the base incorporates the included behaviour as a required part; dashed arrow from base to the included case (both “Update grade” and “Generate Grade Report” include “Verify student ID”). Extend — the base is complete on its own; the extension adds optional behaviour; dashed arrow from the extension to the base (“Apply discount” extends “Checkout”). Generalisation — the specialised case inherits actors, behaviour and meaning; solid line with a hollow triangle pointing to the general case (“Phone order” and “Internet order” generalise “Place order”).

Q7Describe Cockburn's use case levels and say which one is the default for a use case.

Marker wants: three bands; five names; one example per band; sea level as default.

Summary: cloud (enterprise — sell goods, manage inventory) and kite (department — register students). User goal: sea level, one activity of about 2–20 minutes (book an appointment); this is the default. Subfunction: fish (log in, choose a class) and clam (validate login, insert an order line). Writing a sea-level goal with clam-level steps mixes levels and loses marks.

Q8List the fields of a use case description and write a short example for “Book appointment”.

Marker wants: eight fields; a filled example; the ID's traceability purpose.

Name (verb–noun), ID, type (overview/detail; essential/real), primary actor, brief description, importance, stakeholders, triggers (external or temporal), plus relationships and flows. Example: UC-APPT-03 · Book appointment; ID traces to REQ-APPT-014; type detail, essential; primary actor Patient; stakeholders patient, receptionist, manager; importance high (first release); trigger external — the patient requests a slot; brief — reserve a slot with a doctor and confirm it.

Q9Explain the three kinds of flow in a use case description, with one example of each.

Marker wants: three kinds; alternate and exceptional as the two cases of the third; concrete example.

The deck names three kinds; alternate and exceptional are the two cases of the third.
Normal flow — the usual activities: patient chooses a slot, system reserves it, system confirms.
Sub-flow — a decomposed normal step kept separate for clarity (e.g. “verify insurance” used inside booking).
Alternate or exceptional flow — alternate is a legitimate different path: the slot was taken while the patient decided, so the system offers the next three. Exceptional is a failure or blocked condition: the scheduling service is down, so the request is recorded for callback. Alternate is not a failure; exceptional is.

Q10Distinguish essential from real use cases, and overview from detail ones.

Marker wants: two independent axes; the Google Calendar example; why essential is preferred at requirements time.

Essential describes only implementation-independent issues; real commits to technology, design and UI. Overview documents only the overview and relationships; detail documents all parts, including the flow based on the activity diagram. They are independent: a real overview and an essential detail both exist. Example: essential — “receptionist matches the desired appointment time with the available time”; real — “receptionist looks up available dates on Google Calendar”. Essential is preferred at requirements time because a technology choice is a design decision that has not been negotiated.

Q11State the rules for writing the normal flow, then rewrite: “Appointment is booked.”

Marker wants: SVDPI; observer perspective; four parts; a rewritten step with subject and receiver.

Each step is subject–verb–direct object, optionally preposition–indirect object (SVDPI); the initiator and receiver are explicit; it is written from an independent observer's perspective; the set has four parts — the primary actor requests, the system validates, the system processes and may change state, the system returns the result; steps are about equal size and follow KISS. Rewrite: “The patient selects a time slot; the system reserves the slot and returns a confirmation to the patient.”

Q12Describe the process for creating use case descriptions.

Marker wants: priority first; overview/relationships; normal flow; alternate flows; user review; include/extend; repeat.

Choose a high-priority use case and write its overview and relationships parts; describe the normal flow in SVDPI steps; identify and write alternate and exceptional flows; review the description with the user to confirm it is correct; look for include, extend and generalisation opportunities and add or merge cases; repeat until the major use cases are documented. The review is a step: that is where missing actors and alternate flows are found.

Q13What is an activity diagram for, and what do the decision, merge, fork and join symbols mean?

Marker wants: process view across activities; decision vs merge; fork/join pairing; final node.

An activity diagram models a business process: the sequence of activities, the objects flowing between them, and the parties responsible. It can cover a workflow across several use cases, one use case, or one method. Decision (diamond) asks a guarded question and chooses one path; merge (diamond) rejoins alternatives; fork (bar) splits into parallel paths; join (bar) waits for all of them — never a fork without a join. A filled circle starts, a bullseye ends, and swimlanes assign activities to roles.

Q14How do you create a swimlane activity diagram, and what layout rules does the deck give?

Marker wants: the procedure; object flows; decision/parallelism; minimise crossings; left-to-right/top-to-bottom.

Choose the business process to set the scope; review the requirements definition, the use case diagram and the documentation collected; identify the activities and connect them with control flows, object flows and nodes; identify the decisions and the potential parallelism; draw the diagram. Add each activity's input and output as an object flow, put the most important individual or object at the top or left, minimise crossing lines, and lay it out left-to-right and/or top-to-bottom. In the appointment example: patient, reception and doctor lanes, a decision for a free slot whose paths merge before booking, then a final node — and a fork/join if confirmation and the calendar update run in parallel.

Q1Describe the four approaches for identifying objects and classes, and say which one a use-case description invites.

Marker wants: the named parts, not prose.

Textual analysis, brainstorming, common object list, patterns. A use-case description invites textual analysis: nouns become classes or attributes, verbs operations.

Q2For “Appointment”, classify each member as attribute or operation: startTime, reschedule(), status, validateSlot().

Marker wants: the named parts, not prose.

startTime and status are attributes (state); reschedule() and validateSlot() are operations (behaviour). A verb that acts is never an attribute.

Q3Read the multiplicities: Patient 1 — * Appointment, Doctor 1 — * Appointment. What does each end mean?

Marker wants: the named parts, not prose.

One patient has many appointments; each appointment belongs to exactly one patient. Same for doctor. Multiplicity is read at the end nearest the class it counts.

Q4Decide aggregation or composition, with a reason: Ward–Bed, Appointment–Slot.

Marker wants: the named parts, not prose.

Ward–Bed is aggregation: a bed survives the ward closing and can be reassigned. Appointment–Slot is composition: the slot exists for that appointment and is destroyed when it is cancelled.

Q5Why is Person abstract while Doctor is concrete? Give one consequence for a class diagram.

Marker wants: the named parts, not prose.

Person is never instantiated on its own; Doctor and Patient are. The diagram may show Person as a general class with the hollow triangle, and must not show objects of Person.

Q6Apply textual analysis to: “The clinic assigns a doctor to each appointment.” Name the classes, the operation and what the sentence says about multiplicity.

Marker wants: the named parts, not prose.

Classes: Clinic, Doctor, Appointment. Operation: assigns. “Each appointment” gives one doctor to many appointments — multiplicity 1..* at the Appointment end.

Q7List the seven steps and the three checks that follow them.

Marker wants: the named parts, not prose.

(1) textual analysis on descriptions; (2) use SVDPI flow sentences; (3) classes; (4) attributes; (5) operations; (6) relationships with collaborators; (7) visibility and multiplicity. Then (8) review and remove what is unnecessary; (9) incorporate patterns; (10) validate against the use-case diagrams and descriptions.

Q8The description says “the system sends the patient a reminder”, but the class diagram has no Reminder class or link. What is wrong, and which step catches it?

Marker wants: the named parts, not prose.

The structural model is incomplete: the object implied by the description is missing. Step 10 — review and validate, keeping use-case diagrams, descriptions and the class diagram consistent — is the check.
